# Handel Idiomatic Device Catalog

Companion to `handel-lh-vocabulary.md`. The organising fact: **Handel writes for
a public.** The gestures are large, the repetitions are frank, and the effects are
calculated to land in a big room. Where Bach's music rewards study, Handel's
rewards immediate hearing — and generated "Baroque" usually errs toward Bach's
intricacy when Handel's grandeur is simpler and more direct.

## Rhetorical devices

1. **The French overture opening** — dotted rhythms, slow, ceremonial, both hands
   in the same rhythm: `[D4,F#4,A4]dq [D4,F#4,A4]e`. Followed by a fast fugal
   section. The standard way to begin something important.

2. **Terraced dynamics** — `f` for a whole section, `p` for the next. No
   hairpins. The contrast is architectural.

3. **The echo** — a phrase repeated immediately and softer, often by a smaller
   group. The period's dynamic vocabulary in one gesture.

4. **The grand pause before a final cadence** — everything stops, then the
   cadence. Used in every chorus.

5. **Homophonic declamation** — all voices in the same rhythm on one line of text
   or one gesture, then back to counterpoint. Rare enough to be striking.

6. **The long dominant pedal** — eight to sixteen bars, with the upper parts
   accumulating. The build to a final entry.

7. **Unison statement** — everything on one line in octaves, for an opening or a
   sudden emphasis.

## Melodic and structural devices

8. **Sequential repetition** — a four-bar idea stated, then a step or a third
   lower, then again. Handel builds by sequence more than by development, and the
   transposition should be exact.

9. **The ground bass** — a repeating bass pattern (often a descending
   tetrachord) with continuously varied material above. Whole movements are built
   this way.

10. **Ritornello** — a fixed instrumental passage returning between vocal or solo
    episodes, in different keys.

11. **Da capo aria** — A, contrasting B, then A again ornamented by the performer.
    If writing out the return, decorate it.

12. **The written-out cadenza before the final cadence** of an aria.

13. **Melodic sequence over a walking bass** — the two most common ingredients,
    combined; it will fill any transitional passage idiomatically.

14. **Wide, singable intervals** — Handel's lines leap more than Bach's and are
    more vocal in shape: a rising fourth or fifth, then stepwise descent.

15. **Repeated-note declamation** — the same pitch several times in speech
    rhythm, for emphasis.

## Harmonic devices

16. **Circle of fifths** — plain, long, and used without embarrassment.

17. **Suspension chains at cadences** — 4-3 and 7-6, the standard Baroque
    approach.

18. **The Neapolitan sixth** at a moment of pathos in a minor-key aria.

19. **Picardy third** to end a minor-key movement.

20. **Slow harmonic rhythm** — one chord per bar or per two bars, especially in
    the grand choruses. Handel prolongs where Bach moves.

21. **Deceptive cadence to extend a close** — V to vi, then the cadence restated
    and completed.

## Form

The church sonata and the concerto grosso alternate slow and fast. The oratorio
choruses are usually one of: a fugue on a single subject, a homophonic
declamation, or a ground. Choosing which before writing settles most other
decisions.

## What to avoid

- **Bach's intricacy.** If the inner parts are as busy as the outer ones, the
  grandeur is gone.
- **Hairpins and crescendos.** Anachronistic; terrace instead.
- **Fast harmonic rhythm.** Handel's chords last.
- **Varying a sequence.** Exact transposition is the idiom.
- **Complexity for its own sake.** The style's power is in directness.
