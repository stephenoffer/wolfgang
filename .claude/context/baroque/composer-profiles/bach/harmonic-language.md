# J.S. Bach — Harmonic Language

Bach's harmony is not about chords — it is about voices moving. Every chord in Bach is the vertical consequence of horizontal lines going somewhere. This is why his harmony feels simultaneously inevitable and surprising: you can predict the destination (the cadence, the resolution) but never quite predict the route each voice will take to get there. The harmony is generated by counterpoint; the counterpoint is guided by harmony. Neither leads. They are the same thing heard from two angles.

For shared Baroque harmonic vocabulary (figured bass, cadence types, sequences, voice-leading conventions), see [baroque-harmony.md](../../baroque-harmony.md). This file covers what is distinctly Bachian.

## Core Harmonic Character

| Feature | Description | Artistic Function |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Counterpoint-generated harmony | Chords emerge from voice-leading, not the reverse | Every vertical moment has horizontal justification — nothing is arbitrary |
| Pervasive chromaticism | ~25-30% of bass notes are chromatic in mature works | Not decorative but structural — chromaticism IS the emotional argument |
| Suspension chains | 4-3, 7-6, 9-8 chains across all voices | Sustained tension without resolution creates the signature Bach intensity |
| Secondary dominants | V/ii, V/iii, V/IV, V/V, V/vi used freely | Every diatonic chord can be tonicized; the tonal landscape is always shifting |
| Diminished 7th chords | Pivot between keys; dramatic text-painting | The most unstable chord — resolves in four directions, creating harmonic ambiguity |
| Circle-of-fifths backbone | I-IV-vii-iii-vi-ii-V-I as structural frame | Provides gravitational pull even when the surface is highly chromatic |
| Bass as second melody | Bass line has its own contour, peaks, and cadences | The bass is never mere support — it argues, it resists, it leads |
| Tonal ambiguity at phrase level | Brief tonicizations blur key center mid-phrase | The listener is carried forward by uncertainty — resolution feels earned |

## The 16 Harmonic Fingerprints

These are the devices that, taken together, make a passage sound like Bach rather than Handel or Vivaldi.

| # | Device | What It Sounds Like | Typical Context |
|---|--------|--------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Walking chromatic bass | Bass moves by semitone for 3-6 steps | Crucifixus patterns, lament, transitions |
| 2 | 4-3 suspension at every cadence | Penultimate chord has 4th resolving to 3rd | All cadences — this IS the Bach cadence |
| 3 | Chain of 7ths (descending 5ths with suspensions) | Each chord's 7th becomes the next chord's preparation | Sequences, development passages |
| 4 | Tonal answer in fugue (altered 5th-degree entry) | Subject mutated to keep tonic-dominant relationship | All fugues — not a rule but a harmonic decision |
| 5 | Neapolitan 6th in minor cadences | bII6 chord before V | Dramatic moments in minor — the Crucifixus, minor-key fugues |
| 6 | Augmented 6th chords | It+6, Fr+6, Ger+6 approaching V | Intense cadential moments, especially late works |
| 7 | Picardy third | Major I ending a minor-key piece | Nearly universal in Bach's minor-key movements |
| 8 | Cross-relations | Simultaneous natural and sharp/flat in different voices | Text-painting (pain, contradiction); inherited from German tradition |
| 9 | Dominant pedal (8-16 bars) | Bass holds V while upper voices create dissonance | Before final cadences in fugues, preludes, concerto movements |
| 10 | Deceptive cadence (V-vi) | Expected resolution denied | Extends phrases; in cantatas, paints theological "not yet" |
| 11 | Modulation by sequence | Key changes via descending-5ths or ascending-step sequence | Episodic passages in fugues, development in concertos |
| 12 | Enharmonic pivot via dim7 | Same dim7 chord reinterpreted in new key | Late works (WTC II, Musical Offering) — distant modulations |
| 13 | Double suspension (4-3 + 9-8 simultaneous) | Two voices suspend at once | Chorale harmonizations, slow movements |
| 14 | Chromatic voice exchange | Outer voices swap chromatic notes | Creates harmonic color while maintaining voice-leading integrity |
| 15 | Interrupted circle of 5ths | Sequence broken at unexpected point | Redirects harmonic momentum; the listener's expectation bends |
| 16 | Tierce de Picardie as structural pivot | Picardy chord becomes new tonic for following section | Multi-movement works; minor to major key transitions |

## Chorale Harmonization — Where Harmony IS the Art

Bach's 371 chorales are the purest laboratory of his harmonic thinking. The melody is given (the congregation's tune); everything else — bass, inner voices, chromaticism — is Bach's invention.

| Chorale Technique | How It Works | Why It Matters |
|-------------------|-------------|---------------|
| Melody harmonization in context | Same chorale reharmonized differently depending on the text verse | Harmony responds to meaning, not formula |
| Inner voice independence | Alto and tenor have their own melodic logic | No voice is filler — every line rewards attention |
| Fermata as harmonic reset | Each phrase ends with a fermata; the next phrase may begin in a new harmonic color | Modulatory freedom between phrases; each line is a fresh harmonic world |
| Bass leaps to avoid parallels | Bass jumps an octave rather than create parallel 5ths | Physical constraint generates musical invention |
| Chromatic passing tones in inner voices | The alto or tenor moves by semitone where the harmony doesn't require it | Adds warmth and motion without changing the harmonic structure |

```abc
X:1
T:Bach Chorale Harmonization — Chromatic Inner Voices
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:G
V:1
V:2 clef=bass
%% Soprano melody is simple; the inner voices and bass create the richness
[V:1] [BG] [Ac] [GB] [FA] | [EB] [DA] [D^G] [EA] |
[V:2] G, E, D, D, | C, F,, B,, A,, |
%% Note: bass descends G-E-D-D-C-F-B-A — a melody in its own right
%% Inner chromatic motion (the G# in bar 2) creates a brief tonicization of A minor
```

## Suspension Chains — The Emotional Engine

For Bach, the suspension is not a decoration but the primary source of harmonic tension. A chain of suspensions (each resolution becoming the preparation for the next dissonance) creates continuous, unbroken emotional pressure.

| Chain Type | Voice-Leading | Emotional Quality |
|------------|--------------|-------------------|
| 4-3 chain | Upper voice: 4th resolves to 3rd, steps down to next 4th | Pleading, yearning — each resolution opens a new wound |
| 7-6 chain | Upper voice: 7th resolves to 6th, steps up to next 7th | Gentler ache — the descent is slower, more resigned |
| 2-3 chain (bass) | Bass: 2nd resolves to 3rd | Grinding tension from below — the foundation itself is unstable |
| Mixed chain | Alternating 4-3 and 7-6 | Maximum sustained intensity — the Crucifixus effect |

```abc
X:2
T:Bach Suspension Chain — 4-3 Descending
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:Dm
V:1
V:2 clef=bass
%% Each 4-3 suspension: the 4th aches, the 3rd releases, the next preparation begins
[V:1] [D2A2] [^CG] [DA] | [C2G2] [B,F] [CE] | [B,2E2] [A,D] [B,D] | [A,2^C2] [D2F2] |
[V:2] D, A,, D, D, | E, B,, E, A,, | G,, D, G,, G, | A,, A,, D,2 |
%% 4-3 on beat 3 of each bar; the chain descends through D minor
```

## Chromaticism — Not Color but Argument

Bach's chromaticism is structural, not decorative. Where Handel uses chromatic notes as spice (sparingly, for effect), Bach's chromatic lines are load-bearing: remove them and the structure collapses.

| Chromatic Technique | Frequency in Bach | Frequency in Handel | Difference |
|--------------------|-------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Chromatic bass descent | Very high | Moderate | Bach: structural ground bass. Handel: grief gesture |
| Secondary dominants | Very high | Moderate | Bach: every chord is a potential tonic. Handel: V/V mainly |
| Diminished 7th pivots | High | Low-moderate | Bach: modulation tool. Handel: dramatic shock |
| Augmented 6th chords | Moderate (late works) | Rare | Bach integrates them into sequences; Handel reserves them |
| Cross-relations | Moderate | Rare | German tradition vs. Italian-influenced restraint |
| Chromatic inner voices | Pervasive | Low | Bach's inner voices are autonomous; Handel's serve the outer voices |

## Harmonic Rhythm

| Context | Bach's Harmonic Rhythm | Effect |
|---------|----------------------|--------|
| Prelude (keyboard) | 1-2 chords per bar, constant figuration | The harmony unfolds slowly beneath rapid surface motion — meditative |
| Fugue episode | 2-4 chords per bar, sequential | Forward drive through sequence; harmonic rhythm accelerates toward cadence |
| Chorale | 1-2 chords per beat (very fast) | Every beat brings new harmony — maximum density, the richest possible sound |
| Aria (slow) | 2-4 chords per bar | Expressive space for the voice; ritornello provides harmonic anchor |
| Concerto allegro | 1-2 chords per bar in ritornello, faster in episodes | Contrast between stability (ritornello) and adventure (episode) |

## Modulation Patterns

| From | To | Method | Typical Context |
|------|----|--------|----------------|
| I | V | Pivot chord (ii in I = vi in V) | First modulation in binary/fugue exposition |
| i | III | Natural drift to relative major | Minor-key fugues, cantata movements |
| I | vi | Deceptive cadence extended into new key | Development, cantata recitatives |
| i | iv | Circle-of-5ths descent | Sequential episodes |
| Any | remote | Diminished 7th enharmonic reinterpretation | Late works, Musical Offering |
| I | I (return) | Dominant pedal, 4-16 bars | Before final cadence — the longer the pedal, the greater the homecoming |
