# J.S. Bach — Biography

The life of a craftsman who never left central Germany, never sought fame, and yet built a cathedral of sound that contains all of Western music within it. Bach's genius was not the genius of the cosmopolitan — it was the genius of depth. He bored into every form, every technique, every tradition he encountered until he had extracted its essence, then synthesized everything into a language that was entirely his own.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Role | Significance |
|------|----------|------|-------------|
| 1685 | Eisenach, Thuringia | Born | Into a dynasty — over 50 Bachs were professional musicians across seven generations |
| 1695 | Ohrdruf | Orphan, pupil of brother Johann Christoph | Parents died within a year; learned keyboard and copying from his elder brother |
| 1700 | Luneburg | Choirboy, then freelance | Exposure to French organ style (Bohm), North German organ tradition (Reinken) |
| 1703 | Arnstadt | Church organist | First professional post; already in trouble for making the hymns too elaborate |
| 1705 | Lubeck | Pilgrimage to Buxtehude | Walked 250 miles to hear the master; stayed 4 months (was granted 4 weeks) |
| 1707 | Muhlhausen | Church organist | Married Maria Barbara Bach; composed early cantatas (BWV 4, BWV 71) |
| 1708 | Weimar | Court organist, then Konzertmeister | The organ years: Toccatas, Passacaglia in C minor, Orgelbuchlein begun |
| 1713 | Weimar | Discovered Vivaldi's concertos | Transcribed Italian concertos for keyboard — the Italian synthesis begins |
| 1717 | Cothen | Kapellmeister to Prince Leopold | Secular instrumental golden age: Brandenburgs, Cello Suites, WTC Book I, Sonatas & Partitas |
| 1720 | Cothen | Maria Barbara dies | Returned from a trip to find his wife dead and buried |
| 1721 | Cothen | Married Anna Magdalena Wilcke | A singer and copyist; 13 more children; a true musical partnership |
| 1723 | Leipzig | Cantor of St. Thomas School | The final 27 years: cantatas, Passions, Mass in B Minor, Art of Fugue |
| 1729 | Leipzig | Director, Collegium Musicum | Secular concerts at Zimmermann's coffee house; keyboard concertos, orchestral suites |
| 1747 | Potsdam | Visit to Frederick the Great | Improvised a 6-voice fugue on the king's theme; wrote the Musical Offering |
| 1749-50 | Leipzig | Failing eyesight, final works | Art of Fugue left unfinished; two botched eye surgeries; died July 28, 1750 |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### The Family Tradition

Bach was not a lone genius — he was the summit of a family industry. The Bachs were to Thuringian music what the Stradivaris were to Cremona's violins: the profession was the family identity. This mattered because Bach never thought of composition as self-expression. He thought of it as craft — a craft you inherited, practiced, and passed down. The annual Bach family reunions included group improvisation and quodlibets (multi-tune mashups). Music was social, functional, and ongoing.

### Lutheran Theology

Bach's sacred music is not merely "religious music" — it is theology made audible. Lutheran doctrine taught that music was the handmaid of the Word: its purpose was to illuminate scripture, to make the congregation feel the meaning of the text in their bodies. Bach's chorales are sermons in four-part harmony. His Passions are liturgical dramas where the congregation participates through the chorales they already know.

| Theological Concept | Musical Result |
|---------------------|---------------|
| Word-painting (Figurenlehre) | Chromatic descent for "crucified," ascending lines for "risen," dissonance for "sin" |
| Numerology | 14 (B-A-C-H = 2+1+3+8) appears in bar counts, note counts, structural proportions |
| Chorale as congregational anchor | Chorales appear in cantatas, Passions, organ works — the tune the people know grounds the elaboration |
| Suffering as path to grace | Dissonance is not avoided but embraced — the suspension chain IS the theological journey |

### The Synthesis of National Styles

Bach never traveled to Italy or France. He absorbed their music through scores, through colleagues, and through sheer analytical intensity. The synthesis is his greatest achievement.

| Tradition | What Bach Took | Where You Hear It |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| German (Buxtehude, Pachelbel) | Organ virtuosity, chorale-based composition, rigorous counterpoint | Toccatas, chorale preludes, fugues |
| Italian (Vivaldi, Corelli) | Ritornello form, concerto drive, sequential energy, vocal melody | Brandenburg Concertos, keyboard concertos, cantata arias |
| French (Couperin, Lully) | Dance suite forms, ornamental style, dotted rhythms, overture form | English and French Suites, Orchestral Suites, overture-style movements |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Defining Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Apprentice | 1700-07 | Absorbing everything; precocious organ technique; first clashes with authority | Early organ works, BWV 4 (Christ lag) |
| Weimar Organ Master | 1708-17 | Peak organ composition; discovers Italian concerto style; begins systematic exploration | Toccata & Fugue in D minor, Passacaglia in C minor, Orgelbuchlein |
| Cothen Instrumental | 1717-23 | Secular court music; no church duties; instrumental forms perfected | Brandenburgs, WTC I, Cello Suites, Violin Sonatas & Partitas |
| Leipzig Cantor (early) | 1723-29 | Massive cantata production (~2 complete yearly cycles); the two great Passions | St John Passion, St Matthew Passion, ~200 cantatas |
| Leipzig Collegium | 1729-40 | Secular concerts; keyboard concertos; teaching intensifies | Keyboard concertos, Coffee Cantata, Christmas Oratorio |
| Late Summation | 1740-50 | Encyclopedic, abstract, retrospective — summing up all of counterpoint | WTC II, Goldberg Variations, Musical Offering, Mass in B Minor, Art of Fugue |

### What Changes Between Phases

| Aspect | Weimar/Cothen | Early Leipzig | Late Leipzig |
|--------|--------------|---------------|-------------|
| Texture | 2-3 voices, clear, energetic | 4-5 voices, rich, dramatic | 2-6 voices, abstract, dense |
| Chromaticism | Moderate, Italian-influenced | Intense, text-driven | Extreme, structurally motivated |
| Form | Suite, concerto, invention | Cantata, Passion, chorale prelude | Fugue, canon, variation, ricercar |
| Purpose | Court entertainment, teaching | Lutheran liturgy, weekly deadlines | Artistic summation, intellectual legacy |
| Complexity | Brilliant but accessible | Complex but grounded in congregational chorales | Maximum density — Art of Fugue has no designated instrument |

## Legacy

Bach was largely forgotten as a composer for 80 years after his death, remembered only as an organist and pedagogue. The 1829 revival of the St Matthew Passion (by the 20-year-old Mendelssohn) began a reassessment that has never ended. Every subsequent tradition — Classical, Romantic, Modern — has found its own Bach. Mozart studied the fugues. Beethoven called him "the original father of harmony." Brahms, Schoenberg, Stravinsky, and Shostakovich all claimed him as ancestor.
