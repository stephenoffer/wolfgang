# J.S. Bach — Melodic Style

Bach's melodies are not tunes — they are arguments. A short motif enters, and the rest of the phrase is its logical consequence: sequenced, inverted, extended, until every note feels inevitable. Even his vocal lines think like instruments; even his solo lines imply polyphony.

---

## Core Melodic Characteristics

| Feature | Description | Presence |
|---|---|---|
| Fortspinnung | Short head-motif spun into long continuous lines via sequence and development | Constant |
| Motivic saturation | A 2–4 note cell generates melody, bass, inner voices, countersubject | Constant |
| Hidden polyphony | Single-line melodies imply 2–3 independent voices (compound melody) | Very high |
| Instrumental character | Even vocal lines embrace wide leaps, fast runs, full-range writing | Very high |
| Ornamental integration | Turns, mordents, trills are structural, not decorative afterthoughts | Very high |
| Chromatic inflection | Chromatic neighbor tones, passing tones woven into diatonic lines | High |
| Sequence as engine | 2–3 bar melodic units repeated at new pitch levels drive forward motion | Very high |
| Wide range | Melodies frequently span 2+ octaves within a single phrase | High |

---

## Fortspinnung Construction

The characteristic Bach phrase unfolds in three phases — not as a recipe, but as a living process of statement and consequence.

| Phase | Role | Typical Length | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vordersatz (head) | States the motif, plants the key | 1–2 bars | Assertive, rhythmically distinctive |
| Fortspinnung (spin) | Develops via sequence, inversion, fragmentation | 2–6 bars | Forward-driving, harmonically mobile |
| Epilog (cadence) | Cadential arrival, often with trill on penultimate note | 1–2 bars | Resolving, often suddenly still |

The Fortspinnung phase is where Bach's melodic genius lives. The head motif is compressed, inverted, combined with itself — always moving toward a harmonic goal. The listener hears the same shape at different pitch levels and feels transported through key areas without knowing why.

## Interval Vocabulary

| Interval | Typical Usage | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Steps (2nds) | Primary motion — scalewise runs, passagework | Very high |
| 3rds | Arpeggiation within harmony, gentle skips | High |
| 4ths / 5ths | Subject openings, structural leaps that define character | Moderate |
| Minor 6th | Expressive leap — longing, intensity | Moderate |
| Diminished 7th | Dramatic tension, chromatic subjects | Low–mod |
| Octave | Opening gestures, structural markers | Moderate |

Bach subjects often open with a defining leap (4th, 5th, octave) followed by stepwise motion that fills in the harmony. The leap grabs attention; the steps explain it.

## Fugue Subject Types

| Type | Defining Feature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Scalar | Stepwise with one defining leap | WTC I C major |
| Arpeggiated | Outlines a chord; rhythmic profile carries identity | WTC I C minor |
| Chromatic | Half-steps prominent, often lament-inflected | WTC I C# minor |
| Dance-like | Clear metric profile, often gigue-derived | WTC I F major |
| Compact | Short (1–2 bars), dense, endlessly malleable | WTC II F minor |
| Extended | Long (4+ bars), complex, quasi-episodic | Art of Fugue |
| Declamatory | Bold dramatic leaps, rhetorical gestures | St. Anne Fugue |

A good Bach subject contains its own countersubject in potential — the intervals and rhythms of the subject suggest what will sound against it.

## Melodic Techniques in Practice

| Technique | How Bach Uses It |
|---|---|
| Sequence | 3–4 repetitions descending by step or through circle of fifths; always harmonically directed |
| Inversion | Subject turned upside-down — same intervals, opposite direction |
| Fragmentation | Head motif reduced to 2–3 notes, passed rapidly between voices |
| Augmentation | Subject in doubled note values, often in bass at climax |
| Diminution | Subject in halved note values, adding urgency |
| Compound melody | One line leaps between registers to imply soprano + bass simultaneously |
| Stretto | Subject overlaps with its own imitation, compressing time |

## ABC Example — Fortspinnung Melody (D minor)

A head motif (bar 1) spun out through sequential development, arriving at a cadence with trill.

```abc
X:1
T:Bach Melodic Spinning — Fortspinnung in D minor
M:4/4
L:1/16
K:Dm
%% Vordersatz: head motif plants the key
|A,2DE FEDE FAGA FEDE|
%% Fortspinnung: sequential development drives forward
D2FA d2cB A2GF EDCE|D2FG A2Bc d2ef gefd|
%% Epilog: cadential arrival with trill
^c2de fede ^c2A2 d4|
```

The opening cell (A–D–E–F–E–D–E) generates everything that follows. Each sequential repetition starts a step higher, pushing through A minor toward a dominant arrival, then the cadential trill on C# resolves to D.

## ABC Example — Compound Melody (Solo Violin / Cello Style)

A single line implies two voices by alternating between registers — the low notes form a bass line, the high notes form a melody.

```abc
X:2
T:Bach Compound Melody — Solo String Style
M:3/4
L:1/16
K:Dm
|D4 A,4 D4|F4 D4 A,4|E4 A,4 ^C4|D8 z4|
|d4 A4 F4|E4 ^C4 A,4|D4 F4 A4|d8 z4|
```

The ear separates the low register (A, — D — A, — D) from the upper arpeggiation (D–F, E–C#). One instrument, two melodies.

## Ornament Integration

| Ornament | Placement | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Trill | Penultimate note of cadence | Signals cadential arrival — not optional |
| Mordent | Strong beats, especially beat 1 | Rhythmic emphasis, adds bite |
| Turn | Between melodic notes in stepwise motion | Smooths the line, adds elegance |
| Appoggiatura | Before chord tones on strong beats | Expressive dissonance, lean-and-resolve |
| Slide (Schleifer) | Approaching important notes from below | Graceful upward connection |

Ornaments in Bach are part of the melodic argument, not surface decoration. A cadence without its trill is incomplete; a mordent on beat 1 is part of the rhythmic identity. When the ornament is removed, the melody loses meaning, not just beauty.

---

## Phrase Continuity

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| Elision | New phrase begins on the final note of the old phrase — no gap |
| Overlap | Second voice enters with the motif before the first voice finishes |
| Breathing via structure | Rests appear at structural boundaries (cadences), not mid-phrase |
| Perpetual motion | At least one voice maintains sixteenth-note flow at all times |
| Cadential punctuation | Phrases end with clear harmonic arrival + trill, then immediately restart |

Bach's melodies breathe through phrase overlap, not silence. The music pauses only when the structure demands it — at cadences, between sections — and even then, another voice often fills the gap.
