# Edward Elgar — Formal Approach

Elgar works within inherited forms — variation, sonata, concerto, oratorio — and inhabits them with personal intensity. He is not a formal innovator like Strauss or Mahler; he takes the structures Brahms perfected and fills them with Elgarian emotional content. The variation form is his greatest vehicle: the Enigma Variations prove that a single theme can become fourteen different human beings. His symphonies are Brahmsian in architecture, Wagnerian in continuity, and English in emotional character.

## Core Formal Philosophy

| Principle | Description | Contrast with Convention |
|-----------|-------------|------------------------|
| Inherited forms, personal content | Sonata, variation, concerto — used as received but filled with individual emotion | Wagner: forms abolished. Elgar: forms inhabited |
| Variation as character study | Each variation = a specific person, mood, or memory; the form serves portraiture | Brahms: variations explore a theme. Elgar: variations explore people |
| Motto theme across movements | A recurring theme unifies multi-movement works; the motto IS the work's identity | Berlioz: idee fixe. Elgar: the motto is less programmatic, more emotionally unifying |
| Sequence as formal architecture | Sequential passages provide large-scale structural momentum; the climax is earned through sequential build | Common in Tchaikovsky; Elgar adds English weight |
| The promised return | A theme heard early returns transformed at the emotional climax; the return feels inevitable | Cyclical form; Cesar Franck's influence filtered through English temperament |

## Form Types

### Variation Form — The Enigma Model

| Element | Elgar's Treatment | Function |
|---------|-------------------|----------|
| Theme | Simple, memorable, with "enigma" (hidden second theme?) | The emotional seed; every variation grows from this |
| Character variation | Each variation portrays a specific friend; tempo, key, orchestration change | Variation as biography — each person has their own musical world |
| Connecting tissue | Brief transitions between variations; thematic links | Continuity within diversity |
| Finale variation | Extended, developmental, with all previous material gathered | The culmination; the self-portrait (Var. XIV = Elgar himself) |

| Variation | Person | Character | Form within Variation |
|-----------|--------|-----------|----------------------|
| I (C.A.E.) | Alice Elgar | Gentle, loving, domestic | Simple binary; the theme softened |
| IX (Nimrod) | A.J. Jaeger | Noble, rising, the greatest friendship | Slow crescendo; arch form; the emotional peak |
| XI (G.R.S.) | George Robertson Sinclair | Vigorous, the bulldog | Scherzo; the fastest variation |
| XIII (***) | Lady Mary Lygon | Distant, sea-going, mysterious | Tremolo strings = the ship; quotation of Mendelssohn |
| XIV (E.D.U.) | Elgar himself | Grand, processional, comprehensive | Finale; combines themes; the self unveiled |

```abc
X:1
T:Enigma Theme — Designed for Variation
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:Gm
%% Simple, memorable, slightly enigmatic — the seed for 14 character studies
!p!G2A2B2c2|d4 c2B2|A2G2F2E2|D4 G4|
%% Stepwise, no large leaps; easy to transform; the intervals survive any treatment
```

### Sonata Form — The Symphonies

| Element | Elgar's Treatment |
|---------|-------------------|
| Exposition | Broad; first theme = motto (returns across movements); second theme lyrical |
| Development | Sequential; builds through repetition at higher pitch levels; the momentum is gradual |
| Recapitulation | Often abbreviated; the motto returns transformed; not a literal repeat |
| Coda | Extended; the coda IS the emotional destination; sometimes longer than the development |

### Concerto Form

| Work | Form | Special Feature |
|------|------|----------------|
| Violin Concerto Op. 61 | 3 movements; Classical layout | Accompanied cadenza in finale (orchestra participates); the longest Romantic violin concerto |
| Cello Concerto Op. 85 | 4 movements; compressed | Recitative opening; movements flow into each other; chamber-like; the anti-concerto |

```abc
X:2
T:Elgar Symphony Motto — Recurring Theme Across Movements
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:Ab
%% The motto: heard in movement 1, returns transformed in movement 4
!ff!A c e A|G F E D|C2 D2|E4|
%% Strong, processional, unmistakable — designed to be recognized when it returns
```

### Oratorio — The Dream of Gerontius

| Section | Content | Musical Form |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Part I | Gerontius dying; prayers of friends | Through-composed; arioso; choral hymns |
| Interlude | The soul's journey | Orchestral; transformational |
| Part II | The soul before the Angel of the Agony; judgment | Dramatic scenes; the Angel's Farewell as the emotional climax |

## Large-Scale Tonal Architecture

| Work | Tonal Journey | What the Journey Means |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| Symphony 1 | Ab major -> D minor -> Ab major (finale) | The motto's key is the home; the struggle is to return |
| Symphony 2 | Eb major -> C minor -> Eb major | Nobility challenged by grief; the Edwardian world defended |
| Cello Concerto | E minor -> E major -> E minor | Hope glimpsed, then lost; the post-war world |
| Enigma Variations | G minor -> G major (finale) | From the enigmatic private self to the public triumph |

## Proportional Principles

| Principle | Elgar's Practice |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Introduction | Often substantial (Symphony 1: the motto IS the introduction); sets emotional terms |
| Exposition balance | First theme group and second theme group roughly equal in weight |
| Development length | Moderate; Elgar's developments gather force through sequence rather than fragmentation |
| Coda weight | Heavy; the coda is often the emotional destination, not an afterthought |
| Movement proportions | First movement largest; slow movement most intense; finale resolves |
