# Edward Elgar — Cross-References & Influences

## Position in the Tradition

Elgar is the English Late Romantic — the man who gave Britain a symphonic voice that could stand beside Brahms and Tchaikovsky. Before Elgar, English orchestral music was a provincial affair; after Elgar, it was a tradition. He synthesized the Germanic symphonic inheritance (Brahms's architecture, Wagner's continuity) with a specifically English emotional character (pastoral warmth, aristocratic restraint, modal color) and created something that had never existed: an English orchestral school.

---

## Who Influenced Elgar

| Source | What Was Absorbed | Where You Hear It |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| Brahms | Symphonic architecture; motivic development; variation technique; orchestral weight | Symphony 1's formal plan; the Enigma Variations' craftsmanship; the seriousness |
| Wagner | Chromatic harmony; orchestral continuity; emotional intensity; Leitmotif suggestion | Gerontius: through-composed, Wagnerian; the chromatic shadows in major-key passages |
| Schumann | Lyrical warmth; song-like melody; the intimate within the public | The Serenade for Strings; the private Elgar beneath the public surface |
| Tchaikovsky | Sequential technique; emotional directness; the climax built through repetition | Elgar's sequence chains with mounting emotional force |
| Dvorak | Orchestral warmth; pastoral character; structural clarity | The Introduction and Allegro; the Cello Concerto's warmth |
| English choral tradition | Oratorio craft; hymn-tune foundation; the sound of church music | Gerontius; the organ-like sustained chords; the hymn beneath the symphony |
| The English landscape | The Malvern Hills; the Severn valley; walking as compositional practice | The pastoral quality is not borrowed — it is the place where the music was made |

## Who Elgar Influenced

| Successor | What They Took | Where It Led |
|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| Vaughan Williams | The possibility of an English symphonic tradition; pastoral orchestral color | RVW rejected Elgar's Germanism but inherited the English pastoral and the confidence to write English symphonies |
| Walton | Nobilmente character; orchestral brilliance; the public ceremonial voice | Belshazzar's Feast, Symphony 1; Walton is Elgar's spiritual heir in English orchestral writing |
| Britten | Not influence but reaction — Britten defined himself AGAINST Elgar's Edwardian warmth | War Requiem vs. Gerontius; public statement vs. private devotion; but both are English to the core |
| Herbert Howells | The English string tradition; modal harmony; pastoral warmth | Hymnus Paradisi; English choral and orchestral music after Elgar |
| Film composers (British) | The noble string theme; emotional directness; processional character | Every British period drama score; the Elgar sound is England on screen |

## Elgar vs. Brahms

The acknowledged model — and the departure.

| Dimension | Brahms | Elgar |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Form** | Classical forms perfected; abstract; absolute music | Classical forms inhabited; programmatic undercurrent (friends, landscape, memory) |
| **Harmony** | Rich, functional, always tonal; chromatic within discipline | Similar richness, but with English modal inflections (bVII, Mixolydian) |
| **Melody** | Long-spanned but internally structured; motivic | Long-spanned and flowing; nobilmente; less motivically dense |
| **Orchestration** | Warm, middle-register; no extremes; 60 players | Warmer still; more use of upper strings; English string sound; similar restraint |
| **Emotional character** | Autumnal, resigned, deep | Noble, nostalgic, warm; the regret is specifically English |
| **Development** | Motivic: tiny cells generate everything | Sequential: the 2-bar unit repeated at higher levels generates the climax |

## Elgar vs. Wagner

The debt and the distance.

| Dimension | Wagner | Elgar |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Harmony** | Chromatic dissolution; tonal center deferred | Chromatic inflection within tonal stability; the shadow, not the fog |
| **Continuity** | Endless melody; no cadences for entire scenes | Through-composed passages but with clear phrase structure; English periodicity |
| **Orchestration** | 120 players; dense fabric; the orchestra IS the drama | 80 players; warm fabric; the orchestra supports the melody |
| **Form** | Through-composition; form dissolved | Classical forms maintained; through-composition within inherited structures |
| **Emotional stance** | Cosmic: love, death, redemption | Personal: friendship, memory, landscape, the passing of time |
| **Vocal writing** | Declamation over orchestral fabric | In Gerontius: Wagnerian influence, but the vocal line sings more than it declaims |

## Elgar vs. British Contemporaries

| Dimension | Elgar | Vaughan Williams | Holst |
|-----------|-------|-----------------|-------|
| **Harmonic language** | Late Romantic; Germanic; chromatic | Modal; folk-song based; English modality | Modal; Eastern influences; austere |
| **Orchestration** | Full Romantic; Germanic weight | Lighter; pastoral; string-focused | Distinctive; The Planets' orchestral color |
| **Cultural stance** | European; internationalist; Germanic tradition | Nationalist; folk-song collector; English pastoral | Eclectic; mystical; less nationally defined |
| **Melody** | Long-breathed; nobilmente; aristocratic | Folk-song derived; simpler; modal | Rhythmically driven; less lyrical |
| **Emotional range** | Noble, warm, nostalgic, processional | Pastoral, visionary, folk-rooted | Mystical, austere, vivid |

## The Enigma of English Music

Elgar's historical importance: he proved that English music could compete on the European stage.

| Before Elgar | After Elgar |
|-------------|-------------|
| Sullivan (operetta), Parry (choral), Stanford (academic) | Walton, Vaughan Williams, Britten — a full symphonic and operatic tradition |
| English music = provincial, charming, secondary | English music = a real tradition with its own character |
| No English symphony of international stature | Elgar Symphony 1 stands beside Brahms and Tchaikovsky |
