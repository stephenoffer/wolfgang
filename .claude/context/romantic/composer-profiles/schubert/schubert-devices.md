# Schubert Idiomatic Device Catalog

Companion to `schubert-lh-vocabulary.md`, which says the essential thing: the
accompaniment figure holds and the **harmony** provides the surprise. This
catalogue is the rest of the vocabulary.

The organising fact: **Schubert is a song composer even when there is no voice.**
The melody is singable, the phrases are long, and the structures are strophic —
the same material returning with a change of colour rather than being developed.

## Harmonic devices — the heart of the style

1. **Major–minor inflection** — the same melody, the same bass, one note changed
   from major to minor and back. Often on a repeat of a phrase just heard. His
   single most characteristic gesture and it costs one accidental.

2. **The flat submediant (♭VI)** — arriving unprepared. C major side-stepping to
   A-flat major. No pivot, no preparation; the shift itself is the event.

3. **Chromatic mediant relations** — keys a third apart with no common
   dominant: E major from C major, E-flat from G. Schubert modulates by colour
   rather than by function.

4. **The common-tone modulation** — one note held while everything else moves into
   a remote key. The held note makes the impossible sound inevitable.

5. **Prolonged harmony** — a single chord held for two, four, eight bars while the
   accompaniment figure continues. Stillness as expression.

6. **The augmented sixth as a door** — used to leave a key sideways rather than to
   approach a dominant.

7. **Modal mixture throughout** — borrowing freely from the parallel minor in a
   major-key piece (♭III, ♭VI, iv). Not a moment of darkness but a constant
   colour.

8. **A dominant that resolves somewhere unexpected** — set up conventionally, then
   the resolution goes to a chromatic chord instead.

## Melodic devices

9. **The long line** — a melody of eight to sixteen bars with no interior cadence.
   Do not break it into two-bar units.

10. **Repeated note as an anchor** — the melody returns to one pitch again and
    again while the harmony beneath changes its meaning. Deeply his.

11. **The sigh figure** — a falling second, slurred, often repeated in sequence.

12. **Melody doubled at the octave or the sixth** for a strophe's return.

13. **Ornamental turn on a long note** — a decorated arrival, vocal in origin.

14. **The vocal cadence** — the melody falls to the tonic by step from above,
    unhurried, with the accompaniment continuing under it.

## Structural devices

15. **Strophic variation** — the same music repeated with a changed accompaniment,
    a changed mode, or a changed register. Schubert varies by *colour*, not by
    development. This is the alternative to Beethovenian argument.

16. **The three-key exposition** — a sonata exposition visiting a third key before
    settling, instead of moving straight to the dominant.

17. **The long lyrical second subject** — often the emotional centre of the
    movement rather than a foil to the first.

18. **The coda that lingers** — the material recalled quietly rather than
    summarised loudly.

19. **Trudging accompaniment as a whole-piece character** — one figure maintained
    for a movement, establishing a walking, wandering quality. The *Winterreise*
    manner, and it works in instrumental music too.

20. **A silence before a modulation** — a rest, then the music resumes in a
    distant key with no preparation.

## Dynamics

Mostly between `pp` and `mf`. A `ff` is rare and should be shocking. The dynamic
vocabulary is one of *shading*, and long crescendos to a quiet arrival are more
characteristic than crescendos to a loud one.

## What to avoid

- **Changing the accompaniment every phrase.** The commonest error, and it
  destroys the one thing that defines the style.
- **Rushing the harmony.** Prolongation is the medium the surprises sit in.
- **Beethovenian development.** Schubert repeats and re-colours; he rarely
  fragments and argues.
- **Closing every phrase firmly.** His endings are frequently open or elided.
- **Constant forte.** The style lives in the quiet range.
