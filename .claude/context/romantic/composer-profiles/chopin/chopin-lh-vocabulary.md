# Chopin Left-Hand Vocabulary & Phrase Breathing

Chopin's left hand is a *resonating field*, not a rhythm section. It is almost
always wide — the bass note is low and alone, and the chord above it sits an
octave or more higher — and it is held together by the pedal rather than by the
fingers. Generated "Chopin" fails in a specific way: the left hand is too
**narrow** and too **even**. A close-position oom-pah in the middle of the
keyboard is Classical writing with a Romantic tune on top.

Shorthand reminder: each hand's bar sums to the meter. A wide left hand is
written as a low bass note followed by the upper chord, both sustained by pedal:
`Ab1e [Eb3,Ab3,C4]e [Eb3,Ab3,C4]e …`. The reach is not a problem because the
fingers release and the pedal holds.

## LH idiom catalog (adapt pitches to the harmony)

1. **Wide arpeggiated wave** — the nocturne engine. One bass note, then the
   chord unfolding upward across an octave and a half:
   `Ab1e Eb3e Ab3e C4e Eb4e C4e Ab3e Eb3e`. The span from the bass to the top of
   the figure is 15–20 semitones; the *width* is the sound.

2. **Bass note plus displaced chord** — `Bb1q [F3,Bb3,D4]q [F3,Bb3,D4]q` in 3/4.
   The mazurka and waltz shape. The gap between the bass and the chord is the
   point; closing it makes it sound like a folk band.

3. **Rocking tenths** — `Ab1e C3e Ab1e C3e …`. A tenth apart, alternating. Warm
   and open, and the tenth is what makes it Romantic rather than an octave.

4. **Sustained pedal bass under moving inner voices** — `Ab1w // Eb3e F3e Gb3e
   F3e Eb3e F3e Gb3e F3e`. The bass holds for the whole bar while a middle voice
   moves. This is genuine two-voice left-hand writing and it is what most
   generated Chopin is missing.

5. **Chromatic inner-voice descent** — the bass stays, the note above it falls by
   semitone across four bars: `C3 → B2 → Bb2 → A2`. Chopin's most reliable way of
   making a repeated harmony feel like it is going somewhere.

6. **Doubled octaves in the bass** — `[Ab1,Ab2]h [Eb2,Eb3]h`. For weight at a
   climax, or for the ballades' declamatory passages. Rare and heavy; do not use
   it as a default.

7. **Waltz oom-pah-pah, widely spaced** — `Ab1q [C3,Eb3]q [C3,Eb3]q`. The
   textbook figure, but the bass is an octave lower than a Classical waltz would
   put it and the chord is thinner (two notes, not three).

8. **Free arpeggio spanning the whole hand** — `Db1e Ab1e F2e Ab2e Db3e F3e Ab3e
   Db4e`, a two-and-a-half-octave sweep in one bar. The barcarolle and the later
   nocturnes. Written as one gesture, pedalled through.

9. **Repeated chord with a moving bass** — the chord stays put while the bass
   walks underneath it: `Ab1e [C3,Eb3]e G1e [C3,Eb3]e F1e [C3,Eb3]e …`. Motion
   without any change in colour.

10. **The left hand carries the melody** — in the middle register, with the right
    hand accompanying above. Used for a return, or for the middle section of a
    nocturne. The tune sounds completely different an octave lower with a
    different attack, which is the reason to do it.

11. **Silence under a cadenza** — `rest_w`. The right hand runs free and the left
    hand simply stops. Chopin does this at every written-out ornamental flourish;
    keeping the accompaniment going through one is the surest way to make it
    sound mechanical.

## Rubato and the two hands

The left hand keeps time and the right hand is free against it. That is the
classical description of Chopin's rubato and it is worth writing into the notes:
the accompaniment figure should be *regular*, and the melody should have long
notes and short ones that do not line up evenly with it. A melody in even eighths
over an even accompaniment has no rubato to perform.

## Where the idiom changes

- **At the middle section of a ternary form** — usually to something more agitated
  or more chordal, and often lower.
- **At the return** — Chopin ornaments the melody on its return rather than
  changing the accompaniment. Vary the *right* hand there.
- **At the coda** — often to the widest, freest arpeggios of the piece.

Real Chopin repeats its accompaniment far more than intuition suggests: measured
across six mazurkas, distinct left-hand bar-patterns run about **0.15 per bar** —
roughly six of every seven bars reuse a figure already heard. The figure holds;
what changes is the harmony under it and the melody over it.

## Breathing

Chopin's left hand rarely stops mid-phrase, but it thins constantly: the chord
drops a note, the figure loses its top, the bass alone carries a bar. Thinning is
his equivalent of a rest.

## What to avoid

- **A narrow left hand.** If the bass note and the chord above it are less than an
  octave apart, it is not this idiom. Measured on real mazurkas, the left hand's
  own span is routinely 15–22 semitones.
- **No pedal.** Chopin without pedal is a different instrument. The pedal changes
  with the harmony, not with the bar.
- **An even melody.** The whole point of the accompaniment's regularity is to have
  something for the melody to be irregular against.
- **Block triads in root position in the middle register.** The single most common
  way generated Romantic writing collapses into a hymn.
