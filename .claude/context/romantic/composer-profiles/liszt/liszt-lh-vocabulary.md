# Liszt Left-Hand Vocabulary & Phrase Breathing

Liszt's left hand covers the instrument. Its defining property is **range**: a
single bar routinely spans three octaves, and the hand is expected to leap rather
than to stay in one position. The second defining property is that the two hands
frequently swap roles or share one line — the "third hand" effect, where the tune
sits in the middle of the texture with accompaniment above *and* below it.

Generated "Liszt" is usually far too narrow and far too regular. If the left hand
stays within an octave for a whole phrase, it is not this style.

Shorthand reminder: each hand's bar sums to the meter. Wide leaps are written as
ordinary sequential notes; the pedal holds what the fingers release.

## LH idiom catalog (adapt pitches to the harmony)

1. **Three-octave arpeggio sweep** — `Ab1e Eb2e Ab2e C3e Eb3e Ab3e C4e Eb4e`. One
   bar, one gesture, the full compass of the hand's reach with pedal. The
   signature sound.

2. **Leaping bass** — a low octave on the beat, a chord two octaves higher off it:
   `[Ab1,Ab2]q [Eb3,Ab3,C4]q [Ab1,Ab2]q [Eb3,Ab3,C4]q`. The distance is the
   effect; a close-position version of this is a waltz.

3. **The "third hand"** — the melody in the middle register, shared between the
   hands, with figuration above and below it. Written as a left-hand line that
   *is* the tune while the right hand ornaments around it.

4. **Tremolo in the low register** — `[Ab1,Eb2]s ×16`. Orchestral: it is a string
   tremolo transcribed. Under a sustained chordal melody it produces the "orchestra
   at the keyboard" texture the transcriptions are built on.

5. **Repeated octaves, driving** — `[Ab1,Ab2]e ×8` at `ff`. Relentless, and the
   basis of the bravura passages.

6. **Chromatic scale in octaves** — both hands, or the left alone, sweeping across
   two octaves. Transitional, and used to obliterate the previous key.

7. **Widely-spaced sustained chords** — `[Ab1,Ab2,Eb3,Ab3]w`. Cathedral-like, for
   the religious and meditative works. The spacing is open: an octave at the
   bottom, then fifths and octaves above.

8. **Bass alone, unaccompanied, declamatory** — a single melodic line low on the
   instrument with nothing above it. Used at openings and before returns. Sparse
   and rhetorical.

9. **Alternating hands on one figure** — the two hands share a single arpeggio or
   scale, alternating notes, so it can run faster and wider than either could
   alone. A virtuoso device with a real textural result.

10. **Harmonic pedal with chromatic inner motion** — a held low octave while an
    inner voice slides chromatically. Liszt's tension-builder, usually before a
    climax.

11. **Silence, complete** — Liszt drops everything for a bar with a fermata, more
    often than any Romantic composer. The rhetoric is operatic: a pause before
    the next declamation.

## Range as a compositional parameter

Measured on real Liszt, the register span of a movement is routinely five octaves
or more, and it *moves*: passages sit high, then low, then high again. A piece
that lives in a two-octave band around middle C is not in this idiom whatever its
harmony does.

Plan the register explicitly:
- **Openings** are often low and bare.
- **Climaxes** use the top and the bottom simultaneously.
- **Returns** frequently come back an octave higher than the original.
- **Codas** either evaporate upward or descend to the bottom of the instrument.

## Where the idiom changes

- **At every structural point**, and much more often than in Classical writing.
  Liszt's textures are episodic; a new section usually means a new figuration.
- **At a thematic transformation** — the same theme returns in a completely
  different character, and the accompaniment is what transforms it.

## Breathing

Fermatas, general pauses and unaccompanied recitative-like passages. Liszt's
breathing is theatrical rather than metrical — the music stops because a gesture
has finished, not because a four-bar phrase has run out.

## What to avoid

- **A narrow left hand.** The most common failure. If a bar's left hand spans less
  than two octaves in a bravura passage, it is under-written.
- **Even, unvaried figuration.** Liszt's textures change constantly; that is the
  opposite advice from Schubert and it is correct for both.
- **A single dynamic level.** The range is `ppp` to `fff` within a single piece,
  and often within a single page.
- **Four-square phrasing.** Phrases are extended, interrupted and elided. A piece
  in tidy eight-bar units is not this composer.
- **Ignoring the sustain pedal.** These textures are physically impossible without
  it and sound absurd without it.
