# Robert Schumann — Biography

A composer who lived inside literature and music simultaneously. Schumann's piano spoke in characters — Florestan the firebrand, Eusebius the dreamer — and his songs made German poetry sing as never before. The hand injury that ended his performing career forced the music inward; Clara's artistry carried it outward to the world. The final years of mental disintegration shadow everything — but the music itself is the sanest, most feeling utterance of Romantic interiority.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Event | Musical Significance |
|------|----------|-------|---------------------|
| 1810 | Zwickau | Born | Father was a bookseller/publisher — the literary world was home from infancy |
| 1819 | Zwickau | Hears Moscheles play; begins composing | First musical awakenings; already writing small pieces before formal training |
| 1828 | Leipzig | Enters university (law); meets Friedrich Wieck | Piano study begins seriously; the law is abandoned for music within two years |
| 1830 | Leipzig | Commits fully to music; studies with Wieck | Devotes himself entirely to piano virtuosity; Wieck's daughter Clara, age 11, is already a prodigy |
| 1832 | Leipzig | Hand injury (finger mechanism or mercury treatment) | Performing career destroyed; composition becomes the only path; the music turns inward |
| 1834 | Leipzig | Founds *Neue Zeitschrift fuer Musik* | As critic, champions Chopin, Berlioz; invents Florestan/Eusebius as critical alter egos |
| 1835 | Leipzig | Falls in love with Clara Wieck (age 16) | Wieck forbids the relationship; five years of separation produce the greatest piano works |
| 1835-39 | Leipzig | Piano masterworks; court battle for Clara | Carnaval, Kreisleriana, Fantasie Op.17, Davidsbundlertaenze — all written in longing |
| 1840 | Leipzig | Marries Clara (court victory); "Song Year" | 138 songs in one year: Dichterliebe, Frauenliebe, Liederkreis — the dam breaks |
| 1841 | Leipzig | "Symphony Year" | Symphonies 1 "Spring" and 4 (first version); orchestral ambition erupts |
| 1842 | Leipzig | "Chamber Year" | String Quartets Op.41, Piano Quintet Op.44, Piano Quartet Op.47 |
| 1843 | Leipzig | Teaches at new Leipzig Conservatory | Mendelssohn founds the conservatory; Schumann teaches but dislikes institutional life |
| 1844 | Dresden | Moves to Dresden; nervous crisis | Breakdown; months of inability to work; the first serious collapse |
| 1845-49 | Dresden | Recovery; mixed output | Album for the Young, Genoveva (opera), Scenes from Faust, Symphony 2 |
| 1850 | Duesseldorf | Becomes music director | Symphony No.3 "Rhenish"; public duties increasingly difficult as illness progresses |
| 1851 | Duesseldorf | Symphony 3 premiered; begins Violin Concerto | Creative energy alongside growing professional difficulties |
| 1853 | Duesseldorf | Meets 20-year-old Brahms | Publishes "Neue Bahnen" — declares Brahms the future; last great critical act |
| 1854 | Duesseldorf | Suicide attempt (Rhine); enters Endenich asylum | Throws himself into the Rhine in February; rescued; committed to asylum voluntarily |
| 1856 | Endenich | Dies, July 29 | Clara at his bedside; Brahms had kept vigil for months; Clara outlived him by 40 years |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### The Literary Imagination

| Source | What It Gave the Music | Specific Musical Result |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Jean Paul (novelist) | Doppelgaenger theme: a single soul contains multiple characters | Florestan/Eusebius duality; the idea that music can switch character mid-phrase |
| E.T.A. Hoffmann | Music as realm between sanity and vision; the mad artist | Kreisleriana named after Hoffmann's mad Kapellmeister; art as borderland |
| Heinrich Heine | Irony, bitterness beneath lyrical surface; the unreliable speaker | Dichterliebe: the piano's postlude contradicts the voice's apparent emotion |
| Joseph von Eichendorff | Nature mysticism, wandering, the forest as psychic space | Liederkreis Op.39: the self lost and found in landscape; Waldszenen |
| Adelbert von Chamisso | Narrative poetry; the woman's voice; domestic drama | Frauenliebe und -leben: a woman's life story in 8 songs |
| Father's publishing house | Books as native environment; thinking in narratives | Every piano piece has a title, a character, a hidden story |
| Shakespeare | Drama, characterization, psychological complexity | Overture to Julius Caesar; scenes from Hamlet; dramatic instinct |

### The Hand Injury

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| Virtuoso pianist ambition | Composition as sole outlet |
| Music as physical display | Music as interior thought |
| The pianist's body on stage | The piano's voice in private |
| External career path clear | Internal creative path forced open |
| Competition with Clara as performers | Complementary roles: he composes, she performs |

The injury was catastrophe and gift. Without it, Schumann might have been a touring virtuoso who composed on the side. With it, the piano became a private theater of the mind. The hand that could no longer play created music that no hand could easily play — because the music was written for the imagination, not for the fingers.

### Clara

| Dimension | How Clara Shaped Robert's Music |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Performer | She premiered and championed almost everything; his music was written FOR her hands |
| Standard | Her technical brilliance set the difficulty level; Schumann wrote what Clara could play |
| Inspiration | The Fantasie Op.17, the Piano Concerto, Frauenliebe — all addressed to or inspired by her |
| Separation (1835-40) | The years of forbidden love produced the greatest piano works — longing as creative fuel |
| Marriage (1840) | The emotional release of union unlocked the voice: 138 songs in the wedding year |
| Career support | Clara's touring income supported the family; her advocacy kept Robert's music alive |
| Posthumous legacy | Clara edited, performed, and championed Robert's works for 40 years after his death |
| Musical dialogue | Clara was a composer herself; their works quote each other; a musical marriage |

### Mental Illness

| Period | Manifestation | Musical Effect |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| Childhood | Father's mental illness; early awareness of fragility | The shadow is present from the beginning; Schumann always knew |
| 1830s | Periodic depression, fear of madness, anxiety | Dark undercurrents beneath exuberant surface; Florestan covers for Eusebius's despair |
| 1844 | Severe nervous crisis in Dresden; months of paralysis | First major creative interruption; the fear becomes reality |
| 1840s-50s | Auditory hallucinations (musical: a single sustained note, angelic choirs) | Some late works dictated by "angelic voices" — the boundary between creation and illness blurs |
| 1850-53 | Progressive cognitive decline; difficulty conducting | Late works sometimes formally simpler; professional life increasingly impossible |
| 1854 | Suicide attempt; voluntary asylum commitment | Last composition: "Ghost" Variations on a theme he believed angels dictated to him |

### The Neue Zeitschrift fuer Musik

| Role | What It Reveals |
|------|----------------|
| Founder and editor (1834-1844) | Schumann was not just a composer but an intellectual; he THOUGHT about music in words |
| Florestan as critic | Bold, provocative reviews; "Hats off, gentlemen — a genius!" (on Chopin) |
| Eusebius as critic | Gentle, poetic, appreciative; nuanced praise of Schubert, Mendelssohn |
| Davidsbund | Schumann's imaginary "League of David" — artists fighting against philistines |
| Last act: "Neue Bahnen" | The 1853 essay declaring Brahms the chosen successor — prophecy fulfilled |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Key Genres | Defining Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|------------|---------------|
| Piano decade | 1830-1839 | Character pieces, cycles, literary programs; solo piano exclusively | Solo piano | Carnaval, Kreisleriana, Kinderszenen, Fantasie Op.17, Davidsbundlertaenze, Etudes Symphoniques |
| Song year | 1840 | Explosion of Lieder; voice and piano as equal partners | Lieder, song cycles | Dichterliebe, Frauenliebe, Liederkreis Op.24/39, Myrthen |
| Orchestral year | 1841 | Sudden symphonic ambition | Symphonies | Symphony 1 "Spring", Symphony 4 (1st version), Overture/Scherzo/Finale |
| Chamber year | 1842 | Concentrated chamber music | Chamber | String Quartets Op.41, Piano Quintet Op.44, Piano Quartet Op.47 |
| Mixed/late | 1843-1854 | All genres; uneven quality; deepening illness | Opera, concerto, symphony, choral | Piano Concerto, Cello Concerto, Symphony 2 & 3, Genoveva, Scenes from Faust |

## The Schumann Household as Musical Laboratory

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Joint diary | Robert and Clara kept a shared marriage diary — thoughts on music intertwined |
| Musical gifts | Robert wrote pieces for Clara's birthday; Clara wrote pieces for Robert's; mutual quotation |
| Children | Eight children; Album for the Young Op.68 written for their musical education |
| Visitors | Mendelssohn, Liszt, Brahms, Joachim — the Leipzig/Dresden circle was a creative community |
| Tension | Clara's career required touring; Robert's health required stability; the conflict was never resolved |
