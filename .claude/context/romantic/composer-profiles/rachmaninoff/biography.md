# Sergei Rachmaninoff — Biography

The last great Romantic did not choose his time — it chose to leave him behind. Rachmaninoff was born into a Russia of golden church domes and aristocratic estates; he died in Beverly Hills, a concert pianist playing his own music to audiences who heard in it the world that had vanished. His compositional silence after 1917 was not writer's block — it was homesickness so profound that the piano could only repeat what the heart already knew. Every concerto is a letter to a country that no longer existed.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Role | Significance |
|------|----------|------|-------------|
| 1873 | Oneg, Novgorod | Born | Into minor Russian nobility; six children; family fortune dissipated by father |
| 1882 | St. Petersburg Conservatory | Student | Early piano training; family disintegrating; scholarship boy |
| 1885 | Moscow | Pupil of Zverev | Nikolai Zverev's boarding school: brutal discipline, chamber music, exposure to Tchaikovsky |
| 1888 | Moscow Conservatory | Student | Studies with Arensky (harmony), Taneyev (counterpoint); hears Tchaikovsky conduct |
| 1891 | Moscow | Graduates piano | Gold Medal; Piano Concerto No. 1 composed at 18 |
| 1892 | Moscow | Graduates composition | Gold Medal; Prelude in C# minor (Op. 3/2) — instant worldwide fame from one piece |
| 1895 | Moscow | Symphony No. 1 premiere | Catastrophic failure (Glazunov conducting, possibly drunk); devastating reviews |
| 1897–1900 | Moscow | Creative paralysis | Three years of near-total compositional silence; deep depression |
| 1900 | Moscow | Hypnotherapy with Dr. Dahl | Daily sessions: "You will begin to compose... it will be excellent." It worked. |
| 1901 | Moscow | Piano Concerto No. 2 | The recovery made music. Dedicated to Dr. Dahl. Immediate triumph. |
| 1902 | Moscow | Marries Natalia Satina | First cousin; two daughters (Irina 1903, Tatiana 1907); stable family life |
| 1904–06 | Moscow | Conductor, Bolshoi Theatre | Two seasons as opera conductor; conducting becomes second career |
| 1909 | USA | First American tour | Piano Concerto No. 3 composed for tour; plays it with Mahler conducting |
| 1910 | Moscow | Peak maturity | Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom; Etudes-Tableaux Op. 33; The Bells |
| 1915 | Moscow | All-Night Vigil (Vespers) | Greatest choral work; Russian Orthodox liturgical masterpiece |
| 1917 | Stockholm → Copenhagen | Exile begins | Revolution; leaves Russia with one suitcase. Never returns. |
| 1918 | New York | Concert pianist | American career: 40+ concerts per season; grueling touring schedule |
| 1926 | USA | Piano Concerto No. 4 | Cool reception; revised twice (1928, 1941); confidence shaken |
| 1934 | Switzerland | Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini | Villa Senar on Lake Lucerne; the Variation 18 inversion — perhaps the most famous melody he wrote |
| 1936 | USA/Switzerland | Symphony No. 3 | Last symphony; nostalgic, autumnal; mixed reviews |
| 1940 | USA | Symphonic Dances | Final completed work; self-quotation from Vespers; farewell |
| 1943 | Beverly Hills | Dies, March 28 | Melanoma; age 69; buried in Kensico Cemetery, New York — not in Russia |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### The Russian Orthodox Sound

| Liturgical Element | Musical Expression | Where You Hear It |
|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Bell-tower resonance | Open 5ths, tonic pedal, low bass ringing | Piano Concerto No. 2 opening; Isle of the Dead ostinato |
| Znamenny chant | Stepwise descending minor melodies, narrow range | Vespers; Symphony No. 2 slow movement; Vocalise |
| Cathedral acoustics | Sustained harmonies, slow harmonic rhythm, letting sound ring | Bell texture throughout; piano sonata slow passages |
| Basso profondo tradition | Extremely low bass register (to A1 on piano) | LH writing in all piano works |

### The Crisis and Recovery

| Phase | State | Musical Consequence |
|-------|-------|-------------------|
| Pre-crisis (1891–1895) | Youthful confidence | Brilliant, sometimes derivative; echoes Tchaikovsky directly |
| Crisis (1895–1900) | Paralyzed by failure | Almost no composition; self-doubt consumed everything |
| Post-recovery (1900–1917) | Hard-won emotional depth | Piano Concerto No. 2 onward — the music earns its emotion through struggle |
| Exile (1918–1943) | Homesickness, touring exhaustion | Fewer works; each carries enormous weight; nostalgia as defining quality |

### Exile as Amputation

| What Was Lost | Musical Consequence |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Russian language, landscape, liturgy | Self-quotation from earlier works; recycling Russian themes |
| Compositional freedom (touring consumed time) | Only 6 major works in 26 years of exile |
| Audience who understood context | Music becomes more inward, more personal |
| Creative community (Taneyev, Medtner circle) | Isolation; the Symphonic Dances are a solitary farewell |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Defining Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Moscow Youth | 1891–1895 | Brilliant, Tchaikovsky-adjacent, confident | Concerto No. 1, Prelude Op. 3/2, early songs |
| Mature Moscow | 1901–1917 | Peak creativity, depth, Russian Orthodox fusion | Concertos 2-3, Symphonies 2-3, Vespers, The Bells, Isle of the Dead |
| American Exile | 1918–1943 | Sparse output, enormous weight, nostalgic | Concerto No. 4, Paganini Rhapsody, Symphony No. 3, Symphonic Dances |
