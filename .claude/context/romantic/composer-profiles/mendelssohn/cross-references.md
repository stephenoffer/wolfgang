# Felix Mendelssohn — Cross-References

Mendelssohn occupies a unique and contested position in Romantic music. The classicist in the age of revolution. The establishment figure whom the revolutionaries dismissed. The Bach revivalist who shaped how the 19th century understood the 18th. The composer Britain loved most and Germany came to undervalue.

## The Bach Revival

| Aspect | Mendelssohn's Role | Historical Impact |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| St Matthew Passion (1829) | Conducted first performance since Bach's death | Launched the Bach revival; the 18th century re-entered the 19th |
| Bach's formal legacy | Integrated fugal writing into Romantic symphonic music | Showed that counterpoint was alive, not antiquarian |
| Organ works | Composed organ sonatas influenced by Bach's chorale preludes | Kept the organ repertoire connected to Bach's tradition |
| Editorial work | Promoted publication of Bach's works; influenced the Bach Gesellschaft | The scholarly recovery of Bach begins with Mendelssohn's advocacy |

## Mendelssohn vs. Schumann

| Dimension | Mendelssohn | Schumann |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Form | Classical clarity; forms respected | Fantasy and fragmentation; forms stretched |
| Melody | Long, arching, vocal; bel canto | Short, passionate, speech-like; literary |
| Harmony | Functional, diatonic, clear | Adventurous, ambiguous, restless |
| Rhythm | Regular, song-like, symmetrical | Syncopated, displaced, rhythmically complex |
| Orchestration | Transparent, Classical balance | Thick, sometimes muddy; less skilled orchestrator |
| Emotion | Joy, grace, gentle melancholy | Passion, mania, despair; wider extremes |
| Cultural role | Establishment; conductor, educator, institution | Outsider-artist; critic, dreamer, rebel |
| Mutual view | Admired Schumann's originality; promoted his works | Called Brahms the future; valued Mendelssohn's craft |

**Key insight:** They were allies, not rivals. Schumann taught at Mendelssohn's conservatory. They shared a commitment to absolute music against the New German School.

## Mendelssohn vs. the New German School

| Issue | Mendelssohn's Position | Liszt/Wagner Position |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Form | Classical forms are eternal, not exhausted | Classical forms are dead; new forms needed |
| Program music | The overture can be programmatic; the symphony is absolute | The symphonic poem replaces the symphony |
| Harmony | Functional tonality is the foundation | Tonality must be expanded, eventually dissolved |
| Orchestration | Clarity and balance; Classical forces | Expansion, new instruments, massive forces |
| Role of music | Beautiful craft serving emotional expression | Music as philosophical statement, total artwork |
| Cultural politics | Conservative; Leipzig school | Progressive; Weimar school |

**Historical irony:** Mendelssohn's concert overtures (Hebrides, Midsummer Night's Dream) are direct precursors to Liszt's symphonic poems — but Liszt would not acknowledge the debt.

## Mendelssohn vs. Chopin

| Dimension | Mendelssohn | Chopin |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| Piano writing | Brilliant but Classical; the piano as small orchestra | Revolutionary; the piano as its own world |
| Harmony | Functional, clear, diatonic-first | Chromatic, ambiguous, voice-leading-driven |
| Form | Classical structures; Songs Without Words in ternary | Miniatures; free-form nocturnes, ballades |
| Melody | Bel canto in Classical phrases | Bel canto in free, speech-like phrases |
| Shared ground | Both heirs of Italian vocal melody applied to instruments | Mutual respect; performed each other's works |

## Mendelssohn vs. Berlioz

| Dimension | Mendelssohn | Berlioz |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Orchestra | Classical forces, transparent scoring | Expanded forces, experimental color |
| Form | Classical architecture | Program dictates form |
| Melody | Symmetrical, vocal, regular | Asymmetrical, declamatory, long |
| Aesthetic | Beauty, proportion, craft | Drama, spectacle, originality |
| Temperament | Measured, elegant, private | Extreme, theatrical, confessional |
| Meeting | Met in Rome, 1831; mutual bafflement | "Too much counterpoint" (Berlioz); "too much noise" (Mendelssohn) |

## The British Connection

| Aspect | Mendelssohn's Impact on Britain |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| Oratorio tradition | Elijah became the model for Victorian oratorio; Parry and Stanford followed directly |
| Festival culture | Mendelssohn's visits to Birmingham, London, Manchester established the choral festival tradition |
| Orchestral style | The transparent, balanced orchestral sound became the British ideal through Elgar and beyond |
| Conservatory model | The Leipzig Conservatory (where many British composers studied) shaped British musical education |
| Cultural status | Mendelssohn was more honored in Britain than in Germany; Queen Victoria was a devoted admirer |

## The Reputation Problem

| Period | Reputation | Cause |
|--------|-----------|-------|
| 1830s-1847 | Highest: Europe's greatest living composer | Universal acclaim; conductor, composer, educator |
| 1850s-1900 | Declining: "too smooth," "too easy" | Wagner's anti-Semitic attacks; New German School dominance; Victorian over-exposure |
| 1900-1950 | Lowest: "superficial," "salon music" | Modernist rejection of beauty; anti-Semitism in German musicology; the fairy-scherzo caricature |
| 1950-present | Recovering: reassessment of craft and depth | Scholarship reveals the complexity beneath the surface; Op. 80 changes the narrative |

**Critical truth:** The charge of "superficiality" says more about the listener's prejudices than about the music. Mendelssohn's art conceals art — the ease is the achievement, not the limitation.
