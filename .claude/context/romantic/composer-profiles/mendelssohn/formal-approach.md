# Felix Mendelssohn — Formal Approach

Mendelssohn inhabits Classical form with complete sincerity. Sonata form, rondo, variation, ternary — he uses them not as inherited containers but as living structures that organize thought. His development sections actually develop. His recapitulations feel like genuine returns. In an era when Berlioz was breaking form and Liszt was inventing new ones, Mendelssohn proved that Classical architecture could still house Romantic feeling.

## Form Types and Mendelssohn's Treatment

| Form | How Mendelssohn Uses It | Distinguishing Feature |
|------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Sonata-allegro | Textbook clarity: expo-dev-recap with clear thematic groups | Development is motivic and sequential, not dramatic; recap may be abbreviated |
| Sonata-rondo | Finale form; combines rondo returns with sonata development | The Italian Symphony finale (Saltarello) — dancing energy in formal dress |
| Ternary (ABA) | Slow movements; Songs Without Words; scherzi | Middle section contrasts in key and character; return may be varied |
| Rondo | ABACA or ABACABA; clear episode contrast | Each return of A is recognizable; episodes provide tonal and thematic variety |
| Variation | Less common; Variations Serieuses Op. 54 as the masterwork | Progressive intensification; Classical theme with increasingly virtuosic treatment |
| Fugue | Within sonata movements (development sections); oratorio choruses | Real fugal craft — Mendelssohn's Bach training is audible |
| Through-composed overture | The concert overture: narrative form following a program | Hebrides, Calm Sea, Fair Melusine — precursors to the symphonic poem |
| Oratorio | Multi-movement sacred drama: arias, recitatives, choruses | Modeled on Handel and Bach; dramatic but formally structured |

## Sonata Form — Mendelssohn's Approach

| Section | Mendelssohn Practice | Compared to Beethoven |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Introduction | Sometimes present (Scottish Symphony); often omitted | Less dramatic than Beethoven; sets atmosphere |
| First theme | Clear, melodic, singable; establishes key firmly | Less fragmentary than Beethoven; more complete melody |
| Transition | Sequential, rising energy; modulates smoothly to new key | Less combative; the transition flows rather than fights |
| Second theme | Contrasting character (lyrical vs. energetic); new key | Clear thematic contrast; less ambiguity than Brahms |
| Closing group | Cadential confirmation; may introduce new material | Tidier than Beethoven; wraps up cleanly |
| Development | Sequential working of first-theme motifs; keys cycle by 3rds or 5ths | Shorter and less violent than Beethoven; more like Mozart |
| Recapitulation | May be abbreviated or varied; second theme in tonic | Sometimes compressed — Mendelssohn trusts the listener's memory |
| Coda | Can be substantial; sometimes introduces new thematic combination | Celebratory or reflective; never as monumental as Beethoven's |

## The Concert Overture — Mendelssohn's Innovation

Mendelssohn essentially invented the concert overture as an independent orchestral form — a single-movement work with narrative shape but sonata-form structure.

| Overture | Program | Formal Plan |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| Midsummer Night's Dream | Shakespeare's fairy world | Sonata-allegro with fairy-scherzo material |
| The Hebrides (Fingal's Cave) | Scottish seascape | Sonata form with wave-like first theme; storm development |
| Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage | Goethe poem: stillness to motion | Slow intro (calm) followed by Allegro (voyage) and triumphant coda |
| Fair Melusine | Water-sprite legend | Sonata form with undulating accompaniment throughout |
| Ruy Blas | Hugo's drama | Dramatic sonata-allegro; most Beethovenian |

```abc
X:2
T:Mendelssohn Overture Opening — Hebrides Character (wave motif)
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:Bm
!p!B,2 D2 F2 A2|G2 F2 E2 D2|!mp!B,2 D2 F2 B2|A4 G4|
%% The wave motif: rising and falling, providing both theme and texture
%% This dual-function opening is characteristic of Mendelssohn's overtures
```

## Proportional Structure

| Section | Typical Proportion | Character |
|---------|-------------------|-----------|
| Exposition | 35-40% of movement | Generous — Mendelssohn gives themes room to breathe |
| Development | 20-25% | Shorter than Beethoven; efficient, not exhaustive |
| Recapitulation | 25-30% | May be compressed; sometimes abbreviated recap of first theme |
| Coda | 10-15% | Moderate; celebratory or reflective, not monumental |

## Multi-Movement Architecture

### Symphony Layout

| Movement | Tempo | Form | Character |
|----------|-------|------|-----------|
| I | Allegro (or slow intro + Allegro) | Sonata-allegro | Energetic or atmospheric |
| II | Andante or Adagio | Ternary (ABA') or sonata | Lyrical, singing |
| III | Allegretto or Presto | Scherzo (ternary) or minuet | Elfin/fairy OR robust dance |
| IV | Allegro vivace or Presto | Sonata-rondo or sonata | Celebratory, brilliant |

**Notable exception:** The Scottish Symphony plays all four movements without break (attacca) — anticipating Schumann's Fourth and later cyclic works.

### Concerto Innovation

| Feature | Mendelssohn's Approach | Significance |
|---------|----------------------|--------------|
| Cadenza placement | In the development section, not before the coda | The Violin Concerto innovation — integrates the cadenza into the form |
| Movement connection | Movements linked by bridge passages (attacca) | Continuous flow; anticipates later Romantic practice |
| Orchestral role | Orchestra as partner, not adversary | Dialogue rather than combat — the soloist sings with the orchestra |
| Opening | Soloist enters immediately (Violin Concerto) | No long orchestral exposition — gets to the point |

## Oratorio Structure

| Component | Mendelssohn's Treatment |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Overture | Orchestral; sets the dramatic tone |
| Recitative | Accompanied, dramatic; follows Handelian model |
| Aria | Bel canto lyrical; ABA or through-composed |
| Chorus | Fugal, hymn-like, or dramatic; the structural pillars |
| Ensemble | Duets and trios for dramatic dialogue |
| Chorale | Congregational hymn; connects sacred drama to worship |

## Phrase Length and Regularity

| Context | Phrase Length | Symmetry |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Lyrical themes | 8 bars (4+4) | Almost always symmetrical |
| Scherzo fragments | 2 bars | Grouped in 4+4 or 8+8 |
| Transitions | 4 or 6 bars | Sequential extension, less rigid |
| Chorales | 4 bars | Hymn regularity |
| Development | Variable | Sequences in 2-bar or 4-bar units |
