# Gabriel Faure — Cross-References & Influences

## Position in the Tradition

Faure is the hidden bridge of French music. He connects Saint-Saens (Classical clarity, formal elegance) to Debussy (harmonic liberation, color as form) to Ravel (technical mastery, emotional restraint) — and he taught the woman, Nadia Boulanger, who taught half the 20th century. His influence is everywhere and acknowledged nowhere: too French for Germany, too restrained for Romanticism, too tonal for Modernism, too chromatic for conservatism. He exists in a category of one.

---

## Who Influenced Faure

| Source | What Was Absorbed | Where You Hear It |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| Niedermeyer school / Gregorian chant | Modal melody, church modes, smooth voice-leading | All works — the modal inflections ARE the Niedermeyer training |
| Saint-Saens | Modern harmony (Wagner, Liszt); pianistic craft; French clarity | Piano writing, formal elegance, professional model |
| Schumann | Intimate song art; piano as poetic instrument; cross-rhythms | Early songs, piano accompaniment textures |
| Chopin | Nocturne form; flowing piano figuration; harmonic color | Nocturnes, Barcarolles — Faure extends Chopin's nocturne tradition |
| Wagner | Chromatic harmony; continuous modulation; through-composition | Absorbed harmonically but rejected aesthetically (no drama, no display) |
| Liszt | Pianistic idiom; third-related harmony | Occasional — less central than Saint-Saens or Chopin |
| French chanson tradition | Melodic simplicity; strophic song; direct emotional expression | Early melodies; the vocal quality of all his melody |
| Palestrina / Renaissance polyphony | Smooth voice-leading; modal harmony; contrapuntal independence | Late works especially — the String Quartet is Renaissance in its purity |

## Who Faure Influenced

| Successor | What They Took | Where It Led |
|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| Ravel | Harmonic daring, formal precision, emotional restraint, melodic elegance | Ravel was Faure's student at the Conservatoire; absorbed his modal harmony and surpassed his orchestration |
| Nadia Boulanger | Everything — harmonic language, form, aesthetic of clarity and restraint | Boulanger studied with Faure; taught Copland, Carter, Piazzolla, Glass, Quincy Jones — Faure's influence radiates through her to the entire 20th century |
| Lili Boulanger | Lyrical sensitivity, modal harmony, song art | Nadia's sister; died young (1918); her music is the most directly Faurean of any successor |
| Charles Koechlin | Harmonic experimentation, modal richness, orchestral transparency | Koechlin studied with Faure; extended the harmonic language further |
| Florent Schmitt | Orchestral writing, formal ambition | Combined Faure's harmony with larger-scale orchestral forms |
| Roger-Ducasse | Chamber music intimacy, song craft | Direct continuation of Faure's chamber aesthetic |
| Jean Roger-Ducasse | Piano and chamber music | Carried the Faurean aesthetic into the 20th century |

## Faure and Saint-Saens: Teacher and Student

| Dimension | Saint-Saens | Faure |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **Harmony** | Elegant, clear, functional; chromatic but controlled | Modal, continuously modulating; functional but slippery |
| **Form** | Classical clarity; clean proportions; formal perfection | Classical forms inhabited but softened by elided cadences |
| **Melody** | Graceful, balanced, French | Longer, more sinuous, with modal inflection |
| **Orchestration** | Skilled, efficient, sometimes brilliant | Restrained, intimate, deliberately quiet |
| **Emotional range** | Polished, public, occasionally cold | Warm, private, never cold but never demonstrative |
| **Legacy** | Respected but dated; the "establishment" | Revered by musicians; unknown to the public |

## Faure and Debussy: Parallel Paths

Two French composers born 17 years apart, both modal, both anti-Germanic — but fundamentally different.

| Dimension | Faure | Debussy |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **Harmonic language** | Tonal with modal inflection; always a key center | Non-functional; chords as colors; tonality dissolved |
| **Modulation** | Continuous, smooth, through enharmonic pivots | Abrupt juxtaposition; parallel planing; no modulation — just shifting |
| **Melody** | Long, singing, vocal in character | Arabesque, fragmentary, atmospheric |
| **Texture** | Transparent chamber writing; every line sings | Layered, orchestral, timbre-focused |
| **Form** | Classical forms, softened | Free forms; form follows sound |
| **Emotional language** | Private warmth; intimate | Impersonal; sensation rather than emotion |
| **Orchestration** | Restrained, small forces | Masterful, innovative, color-driven |
| **Voice-leading** | Classical rules respected; smooth motion | Parallel motion; rules deliberately broken |

## Faure and Chopin: The Nocturne Tradition

| Dimension | Chopin | Faure |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| Nocturne texture | Melody over arpeggiated LH | Same — Faure continues the Chopin model |
| Harmonic rhythm | Moderate; one chord per bar or two | Similar, but Faure modulates more freely |
| Melodic character | Operatic, ornamented, Italian bel canto | Vocal but less ornamented; French restraint |
| Emotional range | Passionate, anguished, brilliant | Warm, private, never anguished |
| Number of nocturnes | 21 | 13 — spanning his entire career |
| Evolution within set | Significant but contained within Romantic idiom | Vast: from salon romance to near-atonal austerity |

## Faure and Ravel: Teacher and Student

| Dimension | Faure | Ravel |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| **Modal harmony** | Dorian, Mixolydian inflections within tonal context | Same vocabulary, extended with Lydian, octatonic, bitonality |
| **Orchestration** | Restrained, chamber-scale | Brilliant, virtuosic, the greatest orchestrator of the 20th century |
| **Emotional language** | Private, warm, restrained | Cooler, more distant, "Swiss watchmaker" precision |
| **Piano writing** | Idiomatic, flowing, vocal | Virtuosic, orchestral, brilliant |
| **Form** | Classical, softened | Classical, harder-edged, more precisely proportioned |
| **Melody** | Long, sinuous, vocal | Shorter, more defined, folk-influenced |
| **What Ravel took** | Modal harmony, formal clarity, emotional restraint | And added: orchestral mastery, rhythmic precision, Spanish/jazz color |

## Faure and the French Melodie Tradition

| Composer | Contribution to the Melodie | Faure's Position |
|----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| Berlioz | Founded the tradition (Les Nuits d'ete) | Inherited the form |
| Gounod | Established the lyric salon song | Early Faure influenced by this model |
| Duparc | Intensified the harmonic language | Parallel development; Duparc stopped composing young |
| Faure | Perfected the art: ~100 melodies spanning 60 years | The central figure of the tradition |
| Debussy | Transformed it through Impressionist color | Parallel but different: color vs. melody |
| Poulenc | Continued it in the 20th century | Poulenc admired Faure; lighter touch, similar intimacy |

## Comparative Quick Reference

| Trait | Faure | Closest Peer | Key Difference |
|-------|-------|-------------|----------------|
| Modal inflection | Faure | Debussy | Faure: within tonal framework. Debussy: replaces tonal framework |
| Continuous modulation | Faure | Wagner | Faure: smooth, intimate. Wagner: dramatic, chromatic dissolution |
| Avoided cadences | Faure | Debussy | Faure: deflects cadences. Debussy: eliminates them |
| Nocturne piano texture | Faure | Chopin | Faure: more harmonically adventurous. Chopin: more melodically ornate |
| Chamber intimacy | Faure | Brahms | Faure: transparent, French. Brahms: dense, Germanic |
| Long melodic line | Faure | Puccini | Faure: restrained. Puccini: operatic, passionate |
| Late-period austerity | Faure | Late Beethoven | Faure: gradual withdrawal. Beethoven: visionary compression |
