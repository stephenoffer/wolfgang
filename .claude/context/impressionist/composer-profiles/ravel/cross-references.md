# Maurice Ravel — Cross-References & Influences

## Position in the Tradition

Ravel occupies a unique position: the last great master of Classical form who was simultaneously a modernist. He inherited the proportional clarity of Mozart and Haydn, the harmonic elegance of Fauré, the orchestral brilliance of Rimsky-Korsakov, and the coloristic freedom of Debussy — then added jazz, Spanish folk music, and mechanical precision to create a synthesis that remains unequaled for sheer craft. Every note in Ravel earns its place.

## Who Influenced Ravel

| Source | What Was Absorbed | Where You Hear It |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| Fauré | Modal elegance, melodic refinement, formal proportion | Pavane, String Quartet — the foundation of Ravel's restraint |
| Debussy | Non-functional harmony, extended chords, orchestral color | Water imagery (Jeux d'eau), modal scales, pentatonic passages |
| Mozart | Concerto dialogue, Classical formal clarity, proportional balance | Piano Concerto in G — explicitly Mozartian in structure |
| Liszt | Virtuosic piano technique, water effects, transcendental difficulty | Jeux d'eau ("Liszt plus water"), Gaspard de la Nuit |
| Rimsky-Korsakov | Orchestral brilliance, stratified textures, exotic color | Daphnis et Chloé, Rapsodie espagnole |
| Chabrier | Spanish color, rhythmic energy, harmonic daring | Rapsodie espagnole, habanera rhythms |
| Basque folk tradition | Modal melody, dance rhythms, Phrygian inflection | Piano Trio (Basque themes), pervasive Spanish/Iberian DNA |
| Jazz (1928) | Blue notes, swing rhythm, saxophone timbre, stride piano | Concerto in G, Violin Sonata "Blues," Boléro |
| Gershwin | Showed that jazz and concert music could merge | Concerto in G — Ravel called Gershwin "a prince of jazz" |
| Stravinsky | Rhythmic vitality, anti-Romantic clarity | Late works; Ravel admired The Rite of Spring |

## Who Ravel Influenced

| Successor | What They Took | How They Transformed It |
|-----------|---------------|------------------------|
| Messiaen | Extended chord voicing, modal harmony, orchestral color | Systematized into modes of limited transposition |
| Dutilleux | Orchestral stratification, timbral precision, Classical form | Extended into post-tonal frameworks |
| Bernstein | Jazz-classical fusion, orchestral brilliance, rhythmic energy | Transferred to American musical theater and concert music |
| Bill Evans (jazz piano) | Voicings, modal harmony, extended chords | Ravel's piano voicings became foundation of jazz piano |
| Gil Evans | Orchestral arrangement, timbral stratification | Miles Davis collaborations channel Ravel's orchestral thinking |
| Gershwin | Orchestral sophistication (mutual influence) | Gershwin absorbed Ravel's orchestral technique |
| Film scoring (broadly) | Orchestral color, emotional orchestration, fairy-tale textures | Ma Mère l'Oye → Disney orchestral aesthetic |
| Toru Takemitsu | Orchestral delicacy, timbral precision | Combined with Japanese aesthetic |
| George Benjamin | Orchestral color, precise voicing | Extended into spectral-adjacent orchestration |

## Comparative Table: Ravel vs. His Contemporaries

| Dimension | Ravel | Debussy | Stravinsky |
|-----------|-------|---------|------------|
| **Harmonic approach** | Functional extended (ii-V-I with 9ths-13ths) | Non-functional; chords as colors | Pandiatonic, polytonal, neoclassical |
| **Melody** | Clear, singable, periodic | Arabesque, fluid, asymmetric | Rhythmic, fragmented, folk-derived |
| **Orchestration** | Stratified, precise, every note audible | Blended, shimmering, atmospheric | Percussive, rhythmic, color as shock |
| **Form** | Classical forms respected and inhabited | Through-composed, mosaic, triptych | Ritualistic (Rite), neoclassical (later) |
| **Rhythm** | Mechanical precision, dance-based | Fluid, non-metric, flexible | Irregular accents, polyrhythm, vitality |
| **Emotional stance** | Ironic detachment, controlled warmth | Evocative, sensual, dreamlike | Objective, anti-Romantic, ritualistic |
| **Relationship to past** | Respects and updates Classical form | Ignores German tradition | Reinvents past styles (neoclassicism) |
| **Piano writing** | Virtuosic, precise, architectural | Atmospheric, pedal-dependent, coloristic | Percussive, rhythmic, anti-lyrical |
| **Dynamic range** | pp to fff (full range, controlled) | ppp to mf (narrow, subtle) | ppp to ffff (extreme, shocking) |

## Key Relationships

### Ravel and Debussy: The Eternal Comparison

The two are forever paired — unfairly, because their aesthetics are fundamentally different. Ravel crystallizes; Debussy dissolves. Ravel respects form; Debussy reinvents it. Ravel's dissonance is precise; Debussy's is atmospheric.

| Dimension | Debussy | Ravel |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| Chord function | Non-functional — chords float | Functional roots with colorful extensions |
| Voicing | Atmospheric wash; close-position blend | Crystalline; open-position; every note distinct |
| Phrase structure | Asymmetric (5, 7, 11 bars) | Regular periods (4+4, 8+8) |
| Melody | Emerges from texture, inseparable | Rides above texture, always clear |
| Orchestral blend | Instruments merge into new timbres | Instruments stay distinct, stratified |
| Repetition | Avoided — always transforming | Embraced — exact repetition is a technique |
| Climax | Textural complexity, often ppp | Dynamic build; fff is available |
| Cadences | Fadeout, silence, dissolution | Clear cadences; Classical closure |
| Nature | Water as oscillation, mist as whole-tone | Water as virtuosic figuration (Jeux d'eau) |
| Emotional register | Sensuous, dreamlike, evocative | Ironic, precise, sometimes passionate |

### Ravel and Fauré: Teacher and Heir

Fauré taught Ravel composition at the Conservatoire. The inheritance is foundational: modal elegance, melodic refinement, restraint, and the belief that emotion should be felt through craft, not displayed through excess.

| Fauré's Gift | Ravel's Extension |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Modal harmony (Dorian, Lydian inflections) | Extended into full modal palette + Phrygian Spanish color |
| Melodic elegance, long-breathed phrases | Maintained but added virtuosic brilliance |
| Formal proportion, Classical balance | Strengthened into genuine Classical form adherence |
| Emotional restraint, anti-Romantic poise | Deepened into ironic detachment as aesthetic |
| Chamber music as highest art | Chamber writing + supreme orchestral translation |

### Ravel and Jazz: The Two-Way Street

Ravel heard jazz in America (1928) and was fascinated. Gershwin asked Ravel for lessons; Ravel reportedly declined, saying "Why would you want to be a second-rate Ravel when you are already a first-rate Gershwin?"

| Jazz → Ravel | Ravel → Jazz |
|-------------|-------------|
| Blue notes in modal context | Extended chord voicings as standard jazz vocabulary |
| Walking bass patterns | Orchestral stratification → big band arranging |
| Swing rhythm, syncopation | Piano voicings → Bill Evans, jazz piano tradition |
| Saxophone as concert instrument | Impressionist harmony → jazz harmony (modal jazz) |
| Informal rhythmic energy | Formal precision applied to jazz arrangement |

### Ravel and Spain: The Basque Connection

Ravel's mother was Basque; Spanish music was not exotic influence but family inheritance. This gives Ravel's "Spanish" music an authenticity that distinguishes it from Debussy's or Bizet's Orientalist Spain.

| Ravel's Spanish Works | Source |
|----------------------|--------|
| Rapsodie espagnole (1908) | Orchestral; habanera, feria, malagueña |
| Boléro (1928) | Spanish dance form; snare ostinato |
| Don Quichotte à Dulcinée (1932) | Last completed work; Spanish songs |
| Piano Trio (1914) | Basque theme in 8/8 |
| Alborada del gracioso (1905/1918) | Guitar imitation; Phrygian harmony |
