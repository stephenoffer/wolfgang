# Maurice Ravel — Orchestration

Ravel is the supreme orchestrator. Not the loudest, not the largest, but the most precise — every instrument assigned exactly the right note in exactly the right register at exactly the right moment. His orchestrations of his own piano works (Ma Mère l'Oye, Le Tombeau de Couperin, Valses nobles) and of Mussorgsky's Pictures at an Exhibition are masterclasses in timbral thinking. Boléro is not a composition in the traditional sense — it is an orchestration study, the same music repeated while the instruments change.

## Core Orchestral Principles

| Principle | Description | Artistic Function |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Stratification | Each instrument occupies its own register and rhythm | Every layer is audible — the ear can follow any voice |
| Solo as primary color | Melody given to a single solo instrument, not a section | Intimacy, clarity, character — each instrument IS a personality |
| Accumulation over crescendo | Build texture by adding instruments, not by getting louder | Boléro principle: thickness grows while dynamics barely change |
| Piano-to-orchestra translation | Ravel thinks orchestrally even when writing for piano | Every piano piece implies its orchestral version |
| Anti-doubling (except for effect) | Instruments rarely double at unison — each has independent material | Cleaner texture than Wagner or Strauss; no redundancy |
| Color contrast over blend | Adjacent instruments in contrasting timbres, not similar | Flute + trumpet, not flute + oboe — the difference IS the art |
| Percussion as structural element | Percussion (especially snare) carries rhythmic skeleton | Boléro: snare drum is the architecture; melody is decoration |

## Instrument Roles

| Instrument | Ravel's Role | Characteristic Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Flute | Bright pastoral color; high register brilliance | Ma Mère l'Oye solos; Daphnis dawn; extreme high for sparkle |
| Piccolo | Piercing brilliance at climaxes | Top of Boléro accumulation; La Valse frenzy |
| Oboe | Plaintive, expressive solo voice | Slow movements; Le Tombeau de Couperin "Forlane" |
| English horn | Dark warmth, melancholy distance | Rapsodie espagnole; Daphnis pantomime |
| Clarinet | Primary solo voice; warm, flexible, vocal | Boléro early statements; Concerto in G slow mvt |
| Bass clarinet | Dark low warmth, mysterious | Late works; low-register solo color |
| Bassoon | Comic or sardonic; extreme high register exploited | Boléro opening (extreme high C); Ma Mère l'Oye |
| Contrabassoon | Deep rumble, foundation | Rare but structural when present |
| Horn | Warm sustaining, hunting calls, noble | Pavane; Concerto in G; always precisely placed |
| Trumpet | Brilliant, clean; often muted for specific color | Boléro (muted statement); La Valse |
| Trombone | Weight, grandeur, or grotesque | La Valse; Left Hand Concerto; Boléro climax |
| Tuba | Deep foundation at climaxes only | Sparingly used — never routine |
| Harp (1-2) | Color, glissando, harmonic support | Daphnis; Ma Mère l'Oye; essential but not overused |
| Celesta | Sparkle, fairy-tale color | Ma Mère l'Oye; magical moments |
| Piano (orchestral) | Percussive color, rhythmic clarity | Left Hand Concerto; Concerto in G |
| Snare drum | Rhythmic skeleton, ostinato foundation | Boléro — the ENTIRE rhythmic structure |
| Violin I | Melodic leader in tutti; solo for passion | Daphnis; Tzigane; divided for shimmer |
| Violin II | Independent from Vln I — own material | Ravel rarely treats Vln II as Vln I shadow |
| Viola | Warm middle color; melodic | More prominent than in most orchestration |
| Cello | Singing warmth; melodic solo voice | Daphnis love theme; bass melody |
| Double bass | Foundation — pizzicato and sustained | Never busy; structural pizzicato or long pedals |

## The Boléro Principle — Orchestration as Composition

Boléro is the extreme case: identical melody, identical harmony, identical rhythm — for 15 minutes. The ONLY thing that changes is which instruments play. This is Ravel's proof that orchestration IS composition.

| Pass | Instrumentation | Dynamic | Textural Character |
|------|----------------|---------|-------------------|
| 1 | Flute solo | pp | Naked, intimate |
| 2 | Clarinet solo | pp | Warmer, darker |
| 3 | Bassoon (high register!) | p | Strange, nasal, exposed |
| 4 | Eb clarinet | p | Bright, piercing |
| 5 | Oboe d'amore | mp | Sweet, vocal |
| 6 | Trumpet (muted) + flute | mp | Metallic + breathy |
| 7 | Tenor saxophone | mf | Jazz color — unprecedented in classical |
| 8 | Soprano saxophone | mf | Brighter jazz color |
| 9 | Winds + brass combinations | f | Building mass |
| 10-17 | Progressive tutti | f-ff | Full orchestra; melody in unison/octaves |
| 18 | Full orchestra, key change | ff | Explosion — then collapse |

```abc
X:1
T:Ravel — Boléro Accumulation (Pass 1 vs Pass 6)
M:3/4
L:1/8
K:C
V:Fl name="Pass 1: Flute solo"
V:Tp name="Pass 6: Muted Trumpet"
V:Fl2 name="Pass 6: Flute doubling"
%% Same melody — different instruments = different music
[V:Fl] c2d2e2 | f2e2d2 | c2B2c2 | d6 |
[V:Tp] c2d2e2 | f2e2d2 | c2B2c2 | d6 |
[V:Fl2] c2d2e2 | f2e2d2 | c2B2c2 | d6 |
%% Identical pitches. Identical rhythm. But the sound is utterly different.
```

## Piano-to-Orchestra Translation

Ravel's orchestrations of his own piano works demonstrate his principles:

| Piano Texture | Orchestral Translation | Example |
|--------------|----------------------|---------|
| RH melody | Solo woodwind (flute, oboe, clarinet) | Ma Mère l'Oye: piano RH → flute |
| LH bass | Cello + bass pizzicato, or horn sustain | Tombeau: LH bass → horn pedal |
| Middle-register harmony | Divided violas + clarinets | Valses nobles: piano chords → viola+clarinet layers |
| Arpeggiated figure | Harp arpeggios + string tremolo | Ma Mère l'Oye: piano figuration → harp |
| Fortissimo climax | Brass + full strings (NOT just louder winds) | La Valse: piano ff → full orchestra |
| Pianissimo texture | Solo instrument + muted strings | Pavane: piano pp → solo horn + muted strings |

## Textural Stratification

Ravel's orchestra has distinct layers — each audible, each independent.

| Layer | Typical Instruments | Function |
|-------|--------------------|----------|
| Melody layer | Solo wind or solo string | The tune — always clear |
| Harmonic color layer | Horns, muted brass, or sustained winds | The chord — precisely voiced |
| Rhythmic skeleton | Percussion, pizzicato, piano | The pulse — mechanical, regular |
| Bass layer | Cello, bass, contrabassoon | Foundation — spare, not busy |
| Decoration layer | Harp, celesta, high strings | Sparkle, ornament, fairy-dust |

## Dynamic Palette

| Range | Frequency | Context |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| pp | Common | Opening solos, intimate passages |
| p | Common | Standard melodic presentation |
| mp | Common | Moderate texture, emerging fullness |
| mf | Common | Warm, full, confident |
| f | Moderate | Climactic passages, tutti |
| ff | Moderate | Boléro climax, La Valse frenzy |
| fff | Rare but used | Extreme climaxes — Ravel reaches fff where Debussy would not |

## Anti-Patterns in Ravel Orchestration

| Wrong | Why | Instead |
|-------|-----|---------|
| Instruments blending into homogeneous mass | Ravel's instruments stay distinct | Each instrument in its own register with independent material |
| Melody doubled by full section | Ravel prefers solo instrument | Solo flute, solo clarinet, solo violin — not "flutes" |
| Percussion as afterthought | In Ravel, percussion is structural | Snare drum, castanets, cymbals — precisely placed |
| All instruments crescendo together | Ravel builds by addition, not volume | Add instruments one at a time; dynamics increase slowly |
| Thick inner voices obscuring texture | Ravel's middle register is transparent | Sparse inner writing; viola and clarinet, not full wind section |
