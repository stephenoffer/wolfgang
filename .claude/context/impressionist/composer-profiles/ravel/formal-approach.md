# Maurice Ravel — Formal Approach

Ravel loves Classical form. Where Debussy dissolves form into mosaic panels and through-composed flow, Ravel inhabits sonata, concerto, rondo, dance forms, and the variation principle with genuine respect — but fills them with harmony that Haydn could not have imagined. The architecture is 18th century; the materials are 20th century. This tension between Classical proportion and modern color is the core of Ravel's formal art.

## Core Formal Principles

| Principle | Description | Artistic Function |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Classical forms respected | Sonata, rondo, ternary, variation used genuinely | The listener feels structural clarity — knows where they are |
| Modern harmony within old frames | Extended chords, bitonality, modal scales inside Classical structures | Surprise within familiarity — the form is known but the color is new |
| Dance forms as architecture | Waltz, bolero, habanera, minuet — dance IS the form | The body's rhythm organizes the music; the dance is felt physically |
| Repetition as structural principle | Exact repetition of melody/harmony with orchestral variation | Boléro: form IS the accumulation of timbres |
| Proportional balance | Sections relate in balanced ratios (Classical inheritance) | Nothing overstays; the form breathes |
| Clear cadential articulation | Structural boundaries marked by recognizable cadences | Unlike Debussy, Ravel tells you where sections end |

## Form Types

### Sonata Form (Classical Frame, Modern Interior)

Ravel uses genuine sonata form in the String Quartet, Piano Trio, Violin Sonata, and both piano concertos. The expositions have clear first and second themes; the developments modulate; the recapitulations return material in the tonic.

| Section | Ravel's Treatment | Classical Parallel |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Exposition — 1st theme | Clear, periodic, modal or Spanish-inflected | Recognizable as a sonata P-theme |
| Transition | Modulating; sometimes bitonal passage | Functional — genuinely moves to new key area |
| 2nd theme | Contrasting character; often lyrical | S-theme in expected key relationship |
| Development | Reharmonization more than fragmentation; color variation | Shorter than Classical average; recoloring over motivic work |
| Recapitulation | Material returns; second theme in tonic | Genuine recap — Ravel respects the convention |
| Coda | Often brilliant; can be substantial | More substantial than Debussy's evaporations |

| Work | Form | Special Feature |
|------|------|----------------|
| String Quartet, mvt 1 | Sonata-allegro | Two themes; genuine development; clear recap |
| Piano Concerto in G, mvt 1 | Sonata with cadenza | Jazz-inflected themes; Basque rhythmic energy |
| Violin Sonata, mvt 1 | Sonata-allegro | Blues-adjacent second theme |
| Piano Trio, mvt 1 | Sonata-allegro | Basque theme in 8/8 (3+2+3) |

```abc
X:1
T:Ravel — Sonata Form Theme (String Quartet character)
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:F
%% Clear periodic theme — 4+4 bars, identifiable melody, Classical balance
F2G2 A2B2 | c4 A4 | G2A2 B2G2 | F8 |

```

### Ternary Form (A-B-A')

Ravel's most natural large-scale form. The return of A is often orchestrally recolored but melodically similar — unlike Debussy, whose A' returns transformed.

| Work | A section | B section | A' section |
|------|-----------|-----------|------------|
| Pavane pour une infante défunte | Main theme (horn) | Contrasting middle section | Theme returns, same character |
| Ma Mère l'Oye: Pavane | Simple melody | Central episode | Melody returns with added orchestral color |
| Valses nobles, each waltz | First strain | Contrasting strain | First strain returns |
| Tombeau: each movement | Opening material | Central contrast | Return, sometimes abbreviated |

### Dance Forms

Dance is fundamental to Ravel. The rhythm of the body organizes the music.

| Dance Form | Meter | Rhythmic Character | Example Works |
|-----------|-------|-------------------|--------------|
| Bolero | 3/4 | Snare ostinato; melody over mechanical pulse | Boléro |
| Waltz | 3/4 | Elegant 3/4 with rubato; Viennese warmth | La Valse, Valses nobles et sentimentales |
| Habanera | 2/4 | Dotted rhythm (long-short); syncopated sensuality | Rapsodie espagnole, Habanera |
| Minuet | 3/4 | Archaic, courtly, restrained | Menuet antique, Tombeau de Couperin |
| Forlane | 6/8 | Lilting compound meter; Renaissance origin | Tombeau de Couperin |
| Rigaudon | 2/4 or 4/4 | Vigorous, leaping | Tombeau de Couperin |
| Passacaille | 3/4 | Ground bass repeated | Piano Trio, mvt 3 |

```abc
X:2
T:Ravel — Waltz Form (Valses nobles character)
M:3/4
L:1/8
K:G
%% The waltz rhythm is the form — elegant, periodic, danceable
B2d2g2 | f2e2d2 | c2B2A2 | G6 |
%% 4-bar phrase, clear cadence, dance rhythm. Ravel IS the waltz — then he destroys it in La Valse.
```

### Variation/Accumulation Form (Boléro)

Boléro is Ravel's most radical formal invention: the melody and harmony are fixed; only the orchestration varies. The form IS the accumulation of timbral layers.

| Structural Element | Status |
|-------------------|--------|
| Melody | Fixed — Theme A (C major) and Theme B (C minor) alternate |
| Harmony | Fixed — C pedal throughout (until final modulation to E) |
| Rhythm | Fixed — snare drum ostinato unchanged for 15 minutes |
| Orchestration | VARIABLE — the only parameter that changes |
| Dynamic | Gradually increasing through addition of instruments |
| Form | 18 passes of the same 2 themes = gradual textural crescendo |

### Concerto Form

Ravel's two piano concertos are Classical in structure, modern in content.

| Element | Concerto in G | Left Hand Concerto |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|
| Movements | 3 (fast-slow-fast) | 1 (continuous) |
| Form | Sonata / Ternary / Rondo | Through-composed with recapitulation |
| Cadenza | Traditional placement (end of mvt 1) | Integrated into texture |
| Solo-orchestra relation | Dialogue (Mozartian) | Confrontation (Beethovenian) |
| Harmonic language | Jazz-inflected, bitonal, sparkling | Dark, orchestrally dense, powerful |
| Character | Witty, brilliant, light | Epic, nocturnal, one-armed heroism |

## Proportional Balance

| Classical Expectation | Ravel's Practice |
|----------------------|-----------------|
| Balanced phrase lengths | Respected — 4+4, 8+8 standard |
| Sectional proportions | Well-balanced; exposition ≈ recapitulation |
| Coda as brief confirmation | Sometimes extended (Concerto in G, mvt 3) |
| Development as longest section | Often shorter — recoloring is quicker than motivic development |
| Repeat signs | Used (String Quartet expo repeat) — respects Classical convention |

## Transitions and Sectional Boundaries

| Transition Type | Technique | Effect |
|----------------|-----------|--------|
| Modulatory bridge | Genuine modulation from one key area to another | Classical — you hear the key change |
| Rhythmic bridge | Dance rhythm changes to signal new section | The body feels the shift |
| Orchestral bridge | Timbral change marks section boundary | New instrument = new section |
| Cadential closure | Clear cadence (PAC or HC) marks end of section | Structural clarity — the listener knows |
| Attacca | Movements connected without pause | Left Hand Concerto: continuous flow |
