# Monteverdi — Formal Approach

Monteverdi's forms are governed by text and by drama, not by abstract plan, but
unlike Palestrina he has repeating structures available — the ostinato bass, the
refrain, the strophic variation — and he uses them to build spans far longer
than a chain of imitative points could hold together.

---

## The structures he actually uses

| Structure | How it works | Where it belongs |
|-----------|--------------|------------------|
| Ostinato / ground bass | A short bass repeats; the upper line is free above it | Laments; the *Lamento della Ninfa* is the model |
| Strophic variation | The same bass and harmonic frame, new melody each stanza | Solo madrigals, monody |
| Refrain (ritornello) form | An instrumental or choral refrain returns between stanzas | Vespers, larger sacred works |
| Through-composed madrigal | Each phrase of text gets new music; no reprise | The books of madrigals |
| Recitative | Speech-rhythm over a slow bass, cadencing where the sense does | Dramatic works; the invention that makes opera possible |
| Concerted alternation | Solo voices against full ensemble, alternating | Vespers; the concertato principle |

**Ternary appears** — a refrain returning after contrasting material is a real
ABA — but it arrives from the poem's shape, not from a formal template.

---

## How a piece is held together

| Device | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| Repeating bass | Supplies the coherence the free upper line spends |
| Textural alternation | Solo against tutti marks sections as clearly as key change later will |
| Text refrain | The returning line returns with its music |
| Cadence hierarchy | Interior cadences evade; only the final one is allowed to close fully |
| Registral climax | Reserved for the emotional peak of the poem, not the midpoint of the bar count |

---

## Proportions

- Sections follow the poetic line, so they are uneven and often short — 4 to 12
  bars — with the ostinato pieces the exception, running long over a repeating
  4-bar frame.
- The dramatic climax sits late, usually at the poem's turn, and is marked by
  texture and register rather than by volume.
- A lament ends by exhausting its ostinato, not by cadencing decisively — the
  bass simply stops.
