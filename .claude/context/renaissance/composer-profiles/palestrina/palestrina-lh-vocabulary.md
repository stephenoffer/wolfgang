# Palestrina Voice Vocabulary & Phrase Breathing

Palestrina has no left hand. This is vocal polyphony: four to six equal voices,
each singable, each with its own breath. The "accompaniment" concept does not
exist here, and applying it — a tune with something underneath — produces
something that is not this style in any respect.

The catalogue below is therefore of **voice behaviours** rather than keyboard
idioms, and the compiler reads it the same way.

Shorthand reminder: a voice per line, `//` for two voices in one hand, numbered
inner voices beyond that. Palestrina needs three, four and five simultaneous
independent lines — write them.

## Voice idiom catalog

1. **Point of imitation** — one voice states a motif, the others enter with the
   same motif at staggered distances, usually a fourth or fifth apart. Each
   section of text gets its own point. This is the *form*, not a device: a piece
   is a chain of them.

2. **Stepwise motion, overwhelmingly** — real Palestrina is around 80% stepwise.
   A leap is an event. Any line that arpeggiates is out of style.

3. **A leap answered by a step in the opposite direction** — the rule that makes
   the lines singable. After a leap of a fourth or more, the next note steps
   back inside it. Never two leaps in the same direction.

4. **The arch** — a voice rises to a single high point and descends. One peak per
   phrase, and the peak is not repeated.

5. **Suspension and resolution** — a voice holds while the harmony changes
   beneath it, creating a dissonance on the strong beat, then falls by step:
   `G4h~ G4q F4q`. The engine of Renaissance expression, and the only place
   dissonance is allowed on a strong beat.

6. **Passing dissonance on the weak beat** — the other permitted dissonance, and
   only when approached and left by step.

7. **Cambiata** — the one licensed "wrong" figure: down a step, down a third, up
   a step, up a step. A formula worth having in the vocabulary because it sounds
   authentic and nothing else does the same job.

8. **Cadence with a suspended fourth** — the standard close: the upper voice
   suspends over the penultimate bass note, resolves down, and the final is
   reached by step. The leading tone rises.

9. **Voice pairing** — two voices move together in thirds or sixths, answered by
   the other two. Gives contrast without changing the number of parts.

10. **Reduced scoring** — dropping from five voices to two or three for a phrase,
    then returning to full. The Renaissance equivalent of a dynamic change, and
    the principal means of shaping a piece.

11. **Homophony for emphasis** — all voices in the same rhythm on one phrase of
    text, usually a short one, then back to polyphony. Rare and therefore
    striking.

12. **Rests as entries** — a voice is silent for several bars before its next
    point of imitation. Voices enter and drop out constantly, and a texture
    where all voices sing continuously is a texture with no articulation.

## Rhythm

- **No syncopation except in suspensions.** The only off-beat weight is a tied
  note resolving.
- **Longer values predominate**: breves, whole notes and halves, with quarters
  and eighths as motion between them. A surface of running eighths is later
  music.
- **Voices are rhythmically independent** — they do not change note together
  except in a homophonic passage or at a cadence.

## Harmony as a byproduct

The chords are what the counterpoint produces, not what it is built on. Write
each voice as a line and check the verticals; do not write chords and distribute
them. The characteristic sonorities are root-position triads and first
inversions; there are no sevenths as chords, only as suspensions.

Two absolute constraints, and they are what the style *is*:
- **No parallel fifths or octaves between any pair of voices.** Not a stylistic
  preference — a defining rule.
- **No augmented or diminished melodic intervals.** Every leap must be singable.

## Breathing

Each voice breathes at the end of its own phrase, and the phrases are staggered
by design so the texture never stops. That stagger is the whole technique: the
music is continuous while every individual singer rests.

## What to avoid

- **Melody with accompaniment.** The commonest failure, and it is not a matter of
  degree — the style has no such texture.
- **Instrumental figuration.** Arpeggios, broken chords, repeated-note patterns.
  If a human could not sing it in one breath, it does not belong.
- **Dynamics and hairpins.** Not notated in this repertoire.
- **A large range in one voice.** A tenth is the practical limit for a line, and
  most stay within an octave.
- **Chromaticism.** The vocabulary is diatonic with a raised leading tone at
  cadences and the occasional B-flat. Anything more is Gesualdo, not Palestrina.
