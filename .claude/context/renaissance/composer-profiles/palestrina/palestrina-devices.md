# Palestrina Idiomatic Device Catalog

Companion to `palestrina-lh-vocabulary.md`, which says the essential thing: there
is no accompaniment, only equal singable voices. This catalogue is what those
voices do together.

The organising fact: **the piece is a chain of points of imitation**, one per
phrase of text, overlapping so the music never stops. Everything else serves
that.

## Structural devices

1. **Point of imitation** — a motif stated by one voice and answered by the
   others at staggered entries, usually a fourth or fifth apart. Each phrase of
   text gets its own, and the next point begins before the previous one has
   finished. That overlap is the technique.

2. **Overlapping cadences** — as one voice reaches its cadence, another begins the
   next point. A cadence where every voice stops together is an ending, and there
   should be very few.

3. **Reduced scoring** — dropping from five voices to two or three for a phrase,
   then returning. The principal means of shaping, and this repertoire's
   equivalent of a dynamic change.

4. **Voice pairing** — two voices in thirds or sixths answered by the other two.
   Contrast without changing the number of parts.

5. **Homophonic declamation** — all voices in the rhythm of the words for one
   short phrase. Used for the most important line of text, and its rarity is what
   makes it land.

6. **Full-texture climax** — all voices sounding together for the first time,
   placed late.

## Contrapuntal devices

7. **Suspension and resolution** — a voice holds while the bass moves, creating a
   strong-beat dissonance, then falls by step. The only licensed strong-beat
   dissonance, and the engine of the expression.

8. **Suspension chain** — several in succession, the resolution of one preparing
   the next.

9. **Passing dissonance on a weak beat** — approached and left by step. The other
   permitted dissonance.

10. **Cambiata** — down a step, down a third, up a step, up a step. The one
    licensed irregular figure and it sounds unmistakably of the period.

11. **Portamento / anticipation** — the cadential note arrived at early as a short
    note before the beat.

12. **Inversion of the motif** — the same shape upside down, used for a later entry so the point of imitation stays fresh.

13. **Augmentation** of the motif in the lowest voice for a final statement.

## Melodic devices

14. **The arch** — one high point per phrase, approached and left by step. The
    peak is not repeated.

15. **Leap answered by step in the opposite direction** — after any leap of a
    fourth or more, the line steps back inside it.

16. **Stepwise motion overwhelmingly** — around 80% of intervals. A leap is an
    event.

17. **The clivis at a cadence** — the standard cadential formula: the upper voice
    suspends over the penultimate bass, resolves down, and the leading tone rises
    to the final.

## Harmonic devices

18. **Triads in root position and first inversion** — the whole vocabulary. No
    seventh chords except as suspensions.

19. **Musica ficta** — the leading tone raised at cadences, and B-flat used to
    avoid a tritone against F. The only chromaticism in the style.

20. **Plagal cadence for a final Amen** — IV to I, all voices, extended.

21. **Modal rather than tonal** — the piece is in a mode, and the cadence points
    are the mode's own degrees, not a tonic-dominant axis.

## What to avoid

- **Melody with accompaniment.** The style has no such texture.
- **Parallel fifths or octaves between any pair of voices.** Not a preference; a
  defining rule.
- **Augmented or diminished melodic intervals.** Every leap must be singable.
- **Instrumental figuration** — arpeggios, broken chords, repeated patterns.
- **Chromaticism beyond musica ficta.**
- **Dynamics or hairpins.** Not notated in this repertoire.
- **A range wider than a tenth in one voice.**
