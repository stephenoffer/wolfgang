# AI-Generated Music: Self-Critique Guide

How to hear your own output with a critical ear. This document describes the **holistic patterns** that make AI-generated music sound artificial — not individual WMN mistakes (covered in `anti-patterns.md`), but the emergent qualities that arise when technically correct elements combine without a human sensibility behind them.

Use this during self-review: if you recognize these patterns in your output, revise before writing.

---

## The Core Problem: Competent Mediocrity

AI-generated music tends toward a specific failure mode: everything is **adequate and nothing is memorable.** Every phrase has dynamics, every section has ornaments, every theme gets varied — but the result sounds like a committee wrote it. The music is always "doing something" but never **saying** something.

Human composers make bold, asymmetric, sometimes risky choices. AI tends toward safe, balanced, predictable ones. The self-critique question is not "does this have X?" but "would a human composer have written it this way, or does it feel like a checklist was followed?"

---

## Surface-Level Tells

### The Perfectly Balanced Phrase

Every 8-bar phrase has a crescendo in bars 1-4 and a diminuendo in bars 5-8. Every phrase peaks in the middle. Every phrase starts quiet and ends quiet. Real phrases are asymmetric — a crescendo might start on bar 3 and peak on bar 7, two phrases might both crescendo building across a longer span, one phrase might stay quiet throughout.

### The Democracy of Instruments

Every instrument gets "a moment." The flute gets 8 bars, then the oboe, then the clarinet. Real orchestral writing is unequal — the first violin might carry 60% of the melody. The violas might have one glorious moment after 40 bars of accompaniment, and that restraint is what makes it powerful.

### The Texture Slot Machine

Texture changes every 8 bars like clockwork: arpeggio, block chords, tremolo, separated bass, repeat. Changes should be motivated by harmony, melody, or narrative — not a rotation schedule. If you can't articulate WHY a texture change happens at that bar, it's arbitrary.

### Ornament Wallpaper

Ornaments at regular intervals — one per phrase, grace note at the start, trill at the end. Could you remove half and the music would improve? For each ornament: what does it EXPRESS that the bare note does not? If you can't answer, remove it.

### Register Monotony

Everything lives in the middle of the instrument's range. Register is one of the most powerful expressive tools — a theme in the bass feels grounded; the same theme high feels yearning. AI avoids extremes because extremes feel "risky."

### The Missing Silence

Every beat has notes. Every part is doing something. Silence is music's most powerful tool — a rest after a climactic note lets it ring in memory, a beat of silence before a theme entry creates anticipation, a general pause is electrifying. If there's no silence, the music is talking without breathing.

## Structural Tells

### The Development That Doesn't Develop

Theme fragments, sequences, and modulations — all technically correct — but the emotional temperature never rises. Development is narrative: the theme is torn apart by conflict, searching for its way back, growing into something new. If you can only describe your development with technical terms ("fragmented, sequenced, modulated"), you've assembled one, not composed one.

### The Climax That Arrives By Volume Alone

The climax is just the loudest moment — tutti, ff, highest register. Everything gets bigger at once. Great climaxes are multidimensional: harmonic tension, rhythmic tension (sustained note after frantic motion), textural surprise (sudden unison after polyphony), or silence before the final blow. If you muted the dynamics, would the climax still be identifiable from harmony, rhythm, and texture alone?

### Phrase-Level Thinking Only

Each phrase is well-crafted in isolation, but across 32 bars nothing accumulates. The fourth phrase doesn't remember the first. Human composition builds across long spans — a motif in bar 2 pays off in bar 28, an unresolved tendency in phrase 1 creates tension that colors everything until phrase 4.

### The Recapitulation That's Just a Transpose

The recapitulation is the exposition copy-pasted into the home key. A real recapitulation is a *homecoming* — the themes are the same but the journey has changed them. The orchestration might be richer, a passage that was questioning now sounds certain, the transition is shortened because the drama no longer needs it.

### The Coda That Summarizes

The coda restates the main theme one more time and cadences. A real coda transcends — it can be the emotional climax of the entire movement (Beethoven), a gradual dissolution (Mahler), a whispered afterthought (Brahms), or a sudden eruption (Stravinsky). A coda that merely summarizes hasn't earned its place.

### Everything Arrives On Schedule

Modulations appear exactly where structure.json says. Themes enter exactly at the planned bar. The climax falls at 61.8%. Real music has friction — a modulation might arrive a bar early because the harmony is impatient, a theme might be delayed because tension needs to build longer, a climax might overshoot its planned location.

## Harmonic Tells

### The Safe Harmonic Palette

Chord progressions stay within the safest diatonic territory. Modulations use the most obvious pivot chord. No chromatic surprise, no unexpected chord quality, everything resolves on schedule. The deceptive cadence, the borrowed chord, the extended dominant — these are the moments listeners remember.

### Every Dissonance Resolves Immediately

No tension sustains. Every suspension resolves on the next beat. Every dominant goes to tonic. Real music lets dissonance *linger* — Wagner built entire operas on unresolved tension. A chord that "shouldn't" be there but stays for 4 bars can be devastating.

### Harmonic Rhythm That's Too Regular

One chord per bar, every bar, the entire section. Real harmonic rhythm breathes — 4 bars on one chord, then 2 chords per bar as the cadence approaches, then a sudden harmonic acceleration at the climax. The rate of harmonic change is itself an expressive tool.

### The Key Change That Feels Like a Scene Change

Modulations that arrive with a clean cadence in the old key, a pivot chord, and a clean cadence in the new key. Real modulations can be shocking (direct modulation), gradual (chromatic voice leading that slowly shifts the tonal center), or ambiguous (are we in the new key yet? the listener isn't sure, and that uncertainty is the point).

---

## Rhythmic Tells

### Metronomic Regularity

Every note lands exactly on the grid. No syncopation that wasn't planned, no phrase that pushes against the bar line, no moment where the music seems to rush forward or hold back. Real music has rhythmic *personality* — Chopin's rubato, Stravinsky's displaced accents, Brahms's hemiola.

### The Build That's Just Adding Layers

Building intensity by adding one instrument at a time, each entering on beat 1 of a new phrase. Real builds have texture, rhythm, and harmony all intensifying simultaneously — and sometimes the most powerful build is *subtractive* (removing instruments so one voice is left exposed and raw).

### Accompaniment That Ignores the Melody

The left hand plays its pattern. The right hand plays its melody. They coexist but don't interact. In great music, accompaniment *responds* to melody — thickening under a climactic phrase, thinning when the melody needs space, echoing a melodic fragment, pushing against the melody rhythmically.

### Rhythmic Variation That's Just Note Values

Changing from eighth notes to sixteenths isn't rhythmic variation — it's subdivision change. Real rhythmic variation changes the *groove*: syncopation where there was straight rhythm, triplets against duplets, a hemiola that makes 3/4 feel like 2/4, a sudden augmentation that makes time feel suspended.

## Melodic Tells

### The Theme That's Obviously Designed

A theme with a clear hook, clear phrase structure, clear climax placement — all the boxes checked. But it sounds like it was *engineered* rather than *sung*. Real themes feel inevitable, as if they had to exist. The difference is subtle: an engineered theme shows its construction; an inspired theme hides it.

### Every Phrase Ends With a Clean Cadence

Phrase → cadence → breath → next phrase → cadence → breath. Real phrasing is more fluid — phrases can overlap (one ending as the next begins), elide (the cadence of one phrase is the start of the next), or run on (the phrase extends past where the listener expected it to end).

### The Melody That Never Leaves Its Comfort Zone

Staying in a narrow interval range, avoiding large leaps, always resolving stepwise. Real melodies take risks — a sudden octave leap at an emotional peak, a diminished 5th that creates unease, a phrase that descends to the very bottom of the instrument and sits there.

### Counter-Melodies That Are Just Harmony

An inner voice labeled "counter-melody" that's really just chord tones moving in parallel thirds. A real counter-melody has its own personality — its own rhythm, its own contour, its own emotional argument that sometimes agrees with the main melody and sometimes pushes against it.

## Emotional Tells

### Emotion-By-Parameter

Grief = minor, slow, pp, low register. Joy = major, fast, f, high register. These mappings are starting points but real emotional expression is far more nuanced. Grief can be fff and screaming (Shostakovich 8th String Quartet). Joy can be quiet and fragile (Satie Gymnopédies). Triumph can be in a minor key (Beethoven 5th finale is C major but the *struggle* is what makes it triumphant).

### No Sense of Struggle or Discovery

The music always knows where it's going. Every transition is smooth, every modulation is prepared, every arrival is expected. Real music has moments of *searching* — a passage that tries one key and rejects it, a theme that starts to emerge and falters, a harmony that gropes toward resolution. The journey matters as much as the destination.

### The Emotional Arc That's Too Clean

Tension builds steadily, climax arrives, resolution follows. No setbacks, no false climaxes, no moments of ambiguity where the listener doesn't know what to feel. Real emotional arcs have complications — a moment of hope in a tragic passage, a shadow crossing a joyful one, a climax that arrives and then collapses, a resolution that's bittersweet.

### No Humor, Wit, or Playfulness

Music can be funny — Haydn's false endings, Prokofiev's wrong notes, Mozart's comic timing. Humor requires *violating expectations*, which requires first establishing them convincingly. AI tends to establish patterns and then either maintain them or vary them smoothly — it rarely *subverts* them for comedic or dramatic effect.

### No Ugliness or Roughness

Everything is smooth, polished, well-voiced. But some of the most powerful moments in music are *ugly* — Bartók's dissonant clusters, Shostakovich's grinding brass, Stravinsky's pounding asymmetry, the raw sound of a solo instrument at the edge of its range. Ugliness in service of expression isn't a failure; it's honesty.

## Compositional Process Tells

### Assembly Instead of Composition

The section reads like it was built by filling in fields: choose pattern, choose harmonization, add ornaments, set dynamics. Real composition starts from a vision — "this section should feel like dawn breaking" — and every choice flows from that vision. If the compositional process is visible in the output, the process has failed.

### Following the Plan Too Faithfully

The harmony plan says V/V at bar 12, so V/V appears at bar 12. But maybe the music wants to linger on iv for another bar because the melody hasn't finished its thought. Following plans slavishly produces music that serves structure rather than expression. Plans are a starting point — the music should be allowed to push back.

### Defaulting Instead of Choosing

Using `classical_close` voicing because "if unsure, use classical_close." Using `flowing_arpeggio` because it's the first option. Putting an ornament at bar 1 beat 1 because the guide says "phrase entry." Every default is a missed opportunity to make a real choice. If you're reaching for defaults, step back and ask what the moment actually needs.

### The Section That Could Be Anywhere

A section that would work equally well as the opening, the development, or the coda. It's competent music that doesn't know where it lives in the piece. Real sections are *situated* — they know what came before, what comes after, and what role they play in the larger drama.

## The Quota Trap

The most insidious source of AI-generated musical slop is not a bad choice — it's a *counted* choice.

If a system tells you to add 1 ornament per 8 bars, placing ornaments to satisfy that quota IS the wallpaper problem. The same applies to pattern rotation ("change every 4-8 bars"), dynamic markings ("at least one per phrase"), harmonic color moments ("one surprise per section"), and every other countable instruction.

**Count nothing. Ask why.**

- Not "do I have 2+ patterns?" but "does the music need a different pattern here, and why?"
- Not "is there an ornament in this phrase?" but "does this note need an ornament to speak?"
- Not "have I changed the texture in the last 8 bars?" but "does the dramatic moment call for change or continuity?"

Beethoven's 7th Symphony second movement uses essentially one rhythmic pattern for its entire duration. Ravel's Boléro repeats one melody and one pattern for 15 minutes. Chopin wrote nocturnes with 20+ bars of silence in the coda. A system that would flag these as failures has mistaken counting for composing.

The composer who deliberately sustains one pattern for 40 bars — knowing it, choosing it, building tension through it — is composing. The composer who varies patterns every 8 bars because the guideline says to is following instructions. The notes may be identical. The art is completely different.

**Before adding anything — an ornament, a dynamic marking, a pattern change, a textural shift — ask: does the music need this, or do I need to satisfy a requirement?** If the answer is the latter, don't add it.

## The Fundamental Question

After reviewing your output, ask:

**"If a human composer wrote this, would I believe them?"**

Not "is this technically correct." Not "does it follow the style." But: does this music sound like it was written by someone who *felt something* and needed to express it? Does it have personality? Does it take at least one risk? Does it have a moment that couldn't be predicted from what came before?

If the answer is no — if the music is smooth, balanced, correctly varied, and utterly predictable — it needs revision. Not more technique. More *humanity*.
