# Anton Webern — Biography

The most radical compression in the history of Western music. Webern's entire output fits on about three CDs. His Symphony Op. 21 lasts under ten minutes. Some movements are thirty seconds long. Yet this tiny body of work — crystalline, symmetrical, and luminous — became the foundation on which postwar serialism was built. Boulez declared that all music since Webern was irrelevant; Stravinsky spent his final decade working in Webern's shadow. Shot by an American soldier in the chaos of occupied Austria, Webern died at 61 with almost no public recognition.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Event | Musical Significance |
|------|----------|-------|---------------------|
| 1883 | Vienna | Born (Dec 3) | Father: a mining engineer; cultured family; music from childhood |
| 1902 | Vienna | Enters University of Vienna | Studies musicology with Guido Adler; PhD on Heinrich Isaac (Renaissance polyphony) |
| 1904 | Vienna | Begins studying with Schoenberg | The decisive event: joins Berg as Schoenberg's private student |
| 1904–08 | Vienna | Studies with Schoenberg | Learns developing variation, motivic density, contrapuntal rigor |
| 1908 | Vienna | Passacaglia Op. 1 | Graduation piece: tonal, Brahmsian, but already concentrated |
| 1909 | Vienna | Five Movements for String Quartet Op. 5 | Free atonality; extreme brevity; some movements < 1 minute |
| 1910 | Vienna | Six Pieces for Orchestra Op. 6 | Atonal; massive orchestra but used sparingly; extreme dynamics |
| 1911–13 | Various | Conducting career begins | Conducts workers' concerts, opera, private concerts; never prestigious posts |
| 1913 | Vienna | Six Bagatelles for String Quartet Op. 9 | Each movement a miniature world; total = 3 minutes; aphoristic |
| 1914 | Vienna | Five Pieces for Orchestra Op. 10 | The most extreme: mvt. 3 is 19 seconds; mvt. 4 is 6 bars |
| 1914–18 | Military service | WWI | Limited compositional output |
| 1924 | Vienna | Adopts twelve-tone method | Following Schoenberg; applies with maximum rigor |
| 1927 | Vienna | String Trio Op. 20 | First serial work with fully integrated technique |
| 1928 | Vienna | Symphony Op. 21 | The masterpiece: 2 movements; double canon by inversion; palindrome |
| 1930 | Vienna | Quartet Op. 22 | Tenor saxophone + clarinet + violin + piano; unusual ensemble |
| 1934 | Vienna | Concerto Op. 24 | 9 instruments; the BACH motif row; maximum compression |
| 1935–38 | Vienna | Increasingly isolated | Nazi annexation of Austria; "degenerate" music ban; loses conducting posts |
| 1936 | Vienna | Piano Variations Op. 27 | The keyboard masterpiece: palindromes, symmetry, crystalline |
| 1938 | Vienna | String Quartet Op. 28 | BACH-derived row; 3 movements; late serial maturity |
| 1938–45 | Vienna (Modling) | WWII; internal exile | Proofreader for Universal Edition; composes cantatas |
| 1939 | Vienna | Cantata No. 1 Op. 29 | Soprano + orchestra; Hildegard Jone texts; serial + lyrical |
| 1943 | Vienna | Cantata No. 2 Op. 31 | The final completed work; soprano + bass + chorus + orchestra |
| 1945 | Mittersill, Austria | Shot and killed (Sept 15) | Accidentally shot by American occupation soldier; stepping outside after curfew |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### Schoenberg's Teaching (1904–1908)

| What Schoenberg Taught | How Webern Transformed It |
|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Developing variation | Compressed to the absolute minimum: a single motif-transformation IS the piece |
| Motivic density | Maximum: every note derives from the row or its transformation |
| Contrapuntal rigor | Canon as primary formal principle; double canons, mirror canons |
| Twelve-tone method | Applied more strictly than Schoenberg himself; symmetrical rows |

### Renaissance Polyphony (PhD Studies)

| What Webern Studied | Musical Result |
|--------------------|---------------|
| Heinrich Isaac's polyphony | Canonic technique as structural foundation |
| Medieval isorhythm | Rhythmic patterns independent of pitch patterns |
| Franco-Flemish counterpoint | Voice independence; each line a separate entity |
| Structural perfection | Music as architecture; beauty in proportion |

### Alpine Landscape

| Influence | Musical Result |
|-----------|---------------|
| Mountain silence | Silence as primary structural material; rests as long as notes |
| Crystalline air | Purity of sound; single notes in isolation; extreme clarity |
| Wildflowers (Webern was an avid botanist) | Miniature beauty; the smallest form contains the whole |
| Alpine light | Luminosity; ppp sonorities that glow |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Key Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Student/early tonal | 1904–08 | Tonal, Brahmsian, concentrated | Passacaglia Op. 1, early songs |
| Free atonal | 1908–24 | Extreme brevity; aphoristic; pointillistic | Op. 5, 6, 9, 10, 11 |
| Early serial | 1924–33 | Twelve-tone; symmetrical rows; canonic | String Trio Op. 20, Symphony Op. 21, Concerto Op. 24 |
| Late serial | 1934–43 | Serial + lyrical; cantata; Jone texts | Piano Variations Op. 27, Cantatas Op. 29/31 |
