# Sergei Prokofiev — Biography

A child prodigy who wrote an opera at nine, Prokofiev spent his life oscillating between the desire to shock and the desire to sing. He left Russia, conquered Paris, and then — fatally — returned to Stalin's Soviet Union, where his final decade was spent navigating ideological terror while producing some of his greatest music.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Event | Musical Significance |
|------|----------|-------|---------------------|
| 1891 | Sontsovka, Ukraine | Born | Mother was a pianist; he grew up hearing Beethoven and Chopin nightly |
| 1896 | Sontsovka | Composes first piano piece (age 5) | Prodigious talent evident from earliest childhood |
| 1902 | Sontsovka | Composes opera *The Giant* (age 11) | Gliere visits as private tutor; introduces formal composition |
| 1904 | St Petersburg | Enters Conservatory at 13 | Youngest student; studies with Rimsky-Korsakov, Lyadov, Tcherepnin |
| 1908 | St Petersburg | Conservatory years | Already composing shocking piano works; dubbed *enfant terrible* |
| 1914 | London | Meets Diaghilev | Commissions begin; connection to Ballets Russes and Paris modernism |
| 1917 | Petrograd | Russian Revolution | Completes Classical Symphony amid political upheaval |
| 1918 | USA via Japan | Emigrates | Hopes for American career as pianist-composer; mixed reception |
| 1921 | Paris | *Love for Three Oranges* premiered (Chicago) | First major operatic work; grotesque humor established |
| 1923 | Paris | Settles in Paris | Center of emigre artistic life; ballet commissions from Diaghilev |
| 1927 | USSR | First return visit | Soviet authorities court him; he plays concerts; sees "new" Russia |
| 1932 | Paris/Moscow | Increasing Soviet ties | Commissions from Soviet film and theater; Lieutenant Kije |
| 1936 | Moscow | Permanent return to USSR | Leaves Paris family; fatal decision; initially well-treated |
| 1938 | Moscow | *Alexander Nevsky* (film score, Eisenstein) | Patriotic epic; establishes Prokofiev as Soviet cultural asset |
| 1940 | Moscow | *Romeo and Juliet* ballet premiered (Kirov) | Greatest ballet score; lyricism and grotesquerie combined |
| 1941–44 | Evacuation (Nalchik, Alma-Ata) | Wartime works | War Sonatas (6, 7, 8), *War and Peace* opera, Symphony No. 5 |
| 1945 | Moscow | Falls, suffers concussion | Beginning of severe health decline; hypertension, doctors limit work hours |
| 1948 | Moscow | Zhdanov decree | Denounced alongside Shostakovich for "formalism"; forced public apology |
| 1948–53 | Moscow | Final years under pressure | Works simplified under ideological constraint; health worsening |
| 1953 | Moscow | Dies March 5 — same day as Stalin | Death overshadowed; obituary delayed; buried quietly |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### St Petersburg Conservatory (1904–1914)

| Teacher | What Prokofiev Took | What He Rejected |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Rimsky-Korsakov | Orchestral brilliance, clear scoring | Romantic expression, program music |
| Lyadov | Harmonic rigor, formal discipline | Caution, conservatism |
| Tcherepnin | Conducting, theatrical instinct | — |
| Myaskovsky (peer) | Lifelong friendship; symphonic ambition | Myaskovsky's Romantic temperament |

### The Paris Emigration (1920–1936)

| Dimension | What Paris Provided |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Diaghilev commissions | *Chout*, *Le pas d'acier*, *L'enfant prodigue* — ballet as primary form |
| Stravinsky rivalry | Competitive pressure; Neo-Classical clarity as shared aesthetic |
| Cosmopolitan audience | Freedom to experiment; no ideological constraints |
| Isolation from Russia | Nostalgia for Russian melody; eventual vulnerability to Soviet courtship |

### The Soviet Return (1936–1953)

| Phase | Character | Musical Result |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| Honeymoon (1936–41) | Welcomed, well-paid, given apartment | *Romeo and Juliet*, *Alexander Nevsky*, Piano Sonata No. 6 |
| Wartime (1941–45) | Patriotic duty; evacuation; creative peak | Symphony No. 5, War Sonatas 7 & 8, *War and Peace* |
| Zhdanov crisis (1948) | Denounced for "formalism"; public humiliation | Forced simplification; *On Guard for Peace* (cantata) |
| Final decline (1948–53) | Ill, restricted, still composing | Symphony No. 7 (lyrical, simpler), Cello Sonata, Sinfonia Concertante |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Defining Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Enfant terrible | 1908–1918 | Shocking, percussive, brilliant, iconoclastic | Toccata, Sarcasms, Scythian Suite, Piano Concerto No. 1 |
| Emigre modernist | 1918–1936 | Neo-Classical clarity, ballet, Parisian sophistication | Classical Symphony, Piano Concerto No. 3, *Love for Three Oranges*, *Chout*, *The Prodigal Son* |
| Soviet peak | 1936–1945 | Lyricism and power; patriotic and personal combined | *Romeo and Juliet*, *Alexander Nevsky*, Symphony No. 5, War Sonatas |
| Late decline | 1946–1953 | Simplified under pressure; genuine beauty in constraint | Symphony No. 7, Cello Sonata, Sinfonia Concertante |
