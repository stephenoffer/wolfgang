# Arnold Schoenberg — Biography

The most consequential composer of the 20th century — and the most divisive. Schoenberg did not merely compose; he remade the rules. Self-taught in Vienna, he pushed post-Wagnerian chromaticism until tonality broke, spent 15 years composing in free atonality, then invented the twelve-tone method to bring order to the chaos. Exiled to America by the Nazis, he spent his final years in Los Angeles, the inventor of a revolution he could not fully control.

## Life in Brief

| Year | Location | Event | Musical Significance |
|------|----------|-------|---------------------|
| 1874 | Vienna | Born (Sept 13) | Jewish family; father a shoe-shop owner; no musical pedigree |
| 1890s | Vienna | Self-taught in composition | Learned from studying scores (Bach, Beethoven, Brahms, Wagner); brief counterpoint lessons from Zemlinsky |
| 1899 | Vienna | Verklarte Nacht (string sextet) | Post-Wagnerian chromaticism; the last gasp of Romantic tonality; instant scandal and fame |
| 1900–03 | Vienna/Berlin | Gurrelieder composed | Massive cantata; enormous forces; late-Romantic apotheosis |
| 1903 | Vienna | Meets and teaches Berg and Webern | The Second Viennese School begins; the most consequential teacher-student relationships in music |
| 1908 | Vienna | String Quartet No. 2 — final movement atonal | "I feel air from another planet" (Stefan George text); tonality abandoned |
| 1909 | Vienna | Three Piano Pieces Op. 11; Five Orchestral Pieces Op. 16 | Free atonality: no key, no tonal center, no resolution |
| 1909 | Vienna | Erwartung (monodrama) | 30-minute opera; one singer; stream-of-consciousness; Expressionist crisis |
| 1912 | Vienna | Pierrot Lunaire | Sprechstimme; chamber ensemble (fl/picc, cl/bass cl, vn/vla, vc, pno); the Expressionist masterpiece |
| 1915–23 | Vienna | Near silence; searching for method | 8 years of compositional crisis; how to organize atonal music without tonality? |
| 1923 | Vienna | Piano Suite Op. 25 — first twelve-tone work | The method: all 12 chromatic pitches in a fixed order; P/R/I/RI transformations |
| 1924 | Vienna | Technique published and taught | The twelve-tone method becomes a system; Berg and Webern adopt (and adapt) it |
| 1926 | Berlin | Appointed to Prussian Academy of Arts | The pinnacle of institutional recognition in Germany |
| 1933 | Berlin → Paris → USA | Fled Nazi Germany | Dismissed as "degenerate"; reconverted to Judaism in Paris; sailed for America |
| 1934 | Los Angeles | Settles in California | Teaches at UCLA; composes; isolated from European musical life |
| 1936 | Los Angeles | Violin Concerto Op. 36 | Twelve-tone; lyrical; the serial method serving Romantic expression |
| 1942 | Los Angeles | Piano Concerto Op. 42 | Serial; more tonal references; the American works are warmer |
| 1947 | Los Angeles | A Survivor from Warsaw | 12-tone; narrator + male chorus + orchestra; the Holocaust; his most performed work |
| 1946–50 | Los Angeles | String Trio Op. 45; late works | More tonal references; the strict twelve-tone method relaxed |
| 1951 | Los Angeles | Dies (July 13) | 76 years old; triskaidekaphobia (fear of 13) — died on July 13 |

## The Forces That Shaped the Music

### Brahms and Wagner: The Double Inheritance

| From Brahms | From Wagner |
|-------------|-------------|
| Developing variation: a motif transformed through a movement | Chromatic harmony: extended tonality, deferred resolution |
| Thematic unity: everything derives from core material | Leitmotif: short motifs with structural function |
| Chamber music as ideal: concentrated, economical | The endless melody: continuity without cadence |
| Formal rigor: sonata, variation, fugue | Emotional intensity: music as psychological expression |

### The Crisis of Tonality (1908)

| What Happened | Why It Matters |
|--------------|---------------|
| Chromaticism made every note equally likely | If any note can follow any note, there is no "wrong" note |
| Traditional cadences stopped working | V-I felt arbitrary, not inevitable |
| Schoenberg's response: abandon tonality entirely | Free atonality (1908–23): no key, no tonal center |
| The problem: how to create form without tonality? | 15-year search leads to the twelve-tone method |

### American Exile (1933–1951)

| Aspect | Musical Result |
|--------|---------------|
| Distance from European debates | Freedom to relax strict serialism |
| Teaching at UCLA | Pedagogical clarity; textbooks on harmony and counterpoint |
| Emotional distance from European catastrophe | A Survivor from Warsaw: the most personal response |
| California culture | Occasional tonal returns; more accessible late works |

## Career Phases

| Phase | Period | Character | Key Works |
|-------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Late Romantic | 1899–1908 | Post-Wagnerian chromaticism; Brahmsian development | Verklarte Nacht, Gurrelieder, Pelleas und Melisande, Chamber Symphony No. 1 |
| Free atonal | 1908–1923 | No tonality; Sprechstimme; Expressionist; compressed | Op. 11, Op. 16, Erwartung, Pierrot Lunaire, Die gluckliche Hand |
| Twelve-tone | 1923–1936 | Dodecaphonic method; neo-classical forms; serial rigor | Op. 25, Op. 31, Op. 36, Moses und Aron |
| American | 1936–1951 | Serial with tonal references; warmer; occasional tonal works | Violin Concerto, Piano Concerto, A Survivor from Warsaw, String Trio |
