# Beethoven Left-Hand Vocabulary & Phrase Breathing

Beethoven's left hand is not an accompaniment. It is the second half of the
argument — it carries motifs, it interrupts, it drives, and it goes silent at the
moments that matter most. The thing that makes generated "Beethoven" sound wrong
is a left hand that is *decorative*: a pleasant figure ticking along under a tune,
never contradicting it, never taking the initiative.

Shorthand reminder: each hand's bar sums to the meter. A sustained pedal UNDER
figuration is a full-bar first event then the figure (`C2w [C3,G3]e …`) — one
idiom among many, and the most overused.

**Vary it.** A Beethoven phrase typically uses 2–3 LH idioms, and the change
happens where the music means something, not on a timer.

## LH idiom catalog (adapt pitches to the harmony)

1. **Repeated-chord motor** — the engine of the driving movements. Straight
   eighths or sixteenths on one chord: `[C3,Eb3,G3]e ×8`. Relentless, and it
   *accumulates*: what makes it Beethoven rather than Alberti is that it does not
   decorate, it insists. Use under a melody that is itself agitated.

2. **Broken octaves / murky bass** — `C2s C3s C2s C3s …`. The storm engine
   (Op.13 first movement, Op.31/2). Wider and heavier than a tremolo; the two
   notes are an octave apart and the effect is a low growl, not a shimmer.

3. **Rising bass line against a static melody** — quarters or half notes
   climbing chromatically or by step while the right hand holds: `C2h C#2h`,
   `D2h Eb2h`. This is how Beethoven builds tension without getting louder, and
   it is almost absent from generated writing.

4. **The LH takes the theme** — the motif moves to the bass while the right hand
   accompanies. Not a device to save for a fugue: it happens constantly in the
   sonatas, usually at the start of a development. If the piece has a theme and
   the bass never states it, that is a missed opportunity in every movement.

5. **Sforzando punctuation on a weak beat** — `rest_q [C2,C3]q:sfz rest_h`. A
   single loud stab where the metre does not expect one. One of the most
   recognisable Beethoven fingerprints and it costs one note.

6. **Octave doubling of the melody** — both hands on the same line, two or three
   octaves apart, for a bare unison statement. Use at a theme's most emphatic
   return, and at the opening of a movement that means to sound severe.

7. **Sustained low octave under everything** — `[C1,C2]w` held for two or four
   bars while the right hand works above. The bottom of the piano used as a
   *pedal point*, not as a bass line. Enormous weight for one note.

8. **Alberti, deliberately plain** — `C3e G3e E3e G3e ×2`. Beethoven inherits it
   and uses it sparingly, usually in an early or a lyrical work. If a whole
   movement is Alberti it is Mozart's costume, not Beethoven's.

9. **Silence** — `rest_w`. The left hand stops. Beethoven drops the bass out
   before a return, before a fortissimo, and at the moment a phrase is meant to
   hang. A whole bar of nothing underneath is a device, not a gap.

10. **Contrary-motion scales** — both hands moving apart or together in scales
    (`C3e D3e E3e F3e G3e A3e B3e C4e` against a descending right hand). Used at
    transitions and in codas; it is *motion* rather than accompaniment.

11. **Widely-spaced arpeggios** — the hand covering two octaves per bar:
    `C2e G2e C3e E3e G3e C4e G3e E3e`. Open, resonant, and characteristic of the
    later sonatas. Compare with Alberti's narrow oscillation.

12. **Off-beat chords with a silent downbeat** — `rest_e [Eb3,G3]e [Eb3,G3]e
    [Eb3,G3]e …`. The bar has no floor under its first beat. Unsettling in
    exactly the way Beethoven likes.

## Where the idiom changes

- **At a new theme.** The accompaniment identifies the theme; two themes with
  the same accompaniment are one theme.
- **At the start of a development.** Usually to something more agitated, often to
  the LH taking the motif.
- **Approaching a cadence.** Thin toward the bass alone, or stop entirely.
- **At a climax.** Thicker, lower, wider — octaves rather than single notes.

Not on a schedule. Real Beethoven reuses a left-hand figure across a whole phrase
group; measured across six sonata movements, distinct LH bar-patterns run about
0.27 per bar, which means roughly three quarters of bars reuse a figure already
heard.

## Breathing

The left hand rests more than generated writing lets it. A rest under the melody's
phrase-end is how the phrase ends; without it the accompaniment runs on and the
phrase has no punctuation. Aim for the bass to be silent, or to hold one note, at
every structural close.

## What to avoid

- **The same figure for a whole movement.** Beethoven's accompaniments change
  identity when the argument does.
- **A left hand that never takes the tune.** If the bass has no melodic moment in
  a whole piece, the texture is Classical wallpaper, not Beethoven.
- **Perpetual mezzo-forte.** The dynamic range of the left hand alone spans
  `pp` sustained octaves to `ff` broken octaves. Use both ends.
- **A tidy bass that always lands on the root on beat 1.** Inversions, pedal
  points and off-beat entries are what give the bass a will of its own.
