# Ludwig van Beethoven — Melodic Style

Beethoven's melodies are not beautiful lines — they are ideas. A 3-note rhythmic cell, a rising fourth, a dotted figure: these tiny units contain the entire piece in embryo. Where Mozart writes a melody you can sing from beginning to end, Beethoven writes a motif you can develop for 300 bars. The melody is not the destination — the development IS the melody.

---

## Core Melodic Characteristics

| Feature | Description | Presence |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Motivic cell primacy | 2–4 note rhythmic-melodic units generate everything | Constant — THE Beethoven trait |
| Rhythmic identity over pitch | The rhythm of the motif matters more than its notes | Very high (Symphony 5: the rhythm IS the theme) |
| Development as melody | The working-out of a motif across a movement is itself the melodic content | Very high (middle + late) |
| Two theme types | Assertive rhythmic themes (PT) vs. lyrical singing themes (ST) | Constant — the contrast drives sonata drama |
| Wide register | Melodies span 2+ octaves within a phrase; extreme high and low | Moderate (early) → Very high (late) |
| Silence within melody | Rests as structural elements — the gap IS part of the line | Moderate (early) → Very high (late) |
| Declamatory character | Bold leaps, rhetorical pauses — the melody speaks | Present in all periods |
| Motivic saturation | The cell appears in melody, bass, inner voices simultaneously | High (middle) → Extreme (late) |

## The Two Theme Types

Beethoven's sonata movements characteristically pit two contrasting melodic characters against each other.

| Theme Type | Character | Construction | Role |
|------------|-----------|-------------|------|
| Primary theme (PT) | Assertive, rhythmic, angular | Built from a short motivic cell; often unison or octave doubling | States the dramatic argument; provides development material |
| Secondary theme (ST) | Lyrical, singing, broader | Longer phrases, stepwise motion, espressivo | Provides contrast, tenderness; "answers" the PT's assertion |
| Closing theme (CT) | Resolving, often playful | Cadential figures, sometimes derived from PT cell | Confirms the new key; rounds off the exposition |

```abc
X:1
T:Beethoven PT vs. ST Contrast (C minor sonata character)
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:Cm
%% Primary theme: rhythmic cell, angular, assertive
!ff!G,2G,2G,2_E,4 z2|F,2F,2F,2D,4 z2|
%% Secondary theme: lyrical, singing, Eb major
K:Eb
!p!B,2C2D2_E2|F4 _E2D2|
```

## Motivic Cell Development

The cell is Beethoven's melodic DNA. It is presented, then systematically transformed through the movement.

### Transformation Techniques

| Technique | What Happens | Dramatic Function |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Transposition | Cell at new pitch levels in sequence | Builds momentum; explores key areas |
| Fragmentation | Cell reduced to 2 notes, then 1 | Compression — maximum intensity before structural arrival |
| Augmentation | Cell in longer note values | Weight, gravitas; often at recapitulation |
| Diminution | Cell in shorter note values | Urgency; accelerating development |
| Inversion | Cell upside-down | Fresh perspective on familiar material |
| Rhythmic variation | Same pitches, new rhythm | Transforms character without losing identity |
| Register transfer | Cell moved to bass, inner voice, or extreme register | The argument spreads to all participants |
| Counterpoint | Cell set against itself or a contrasting motif | Two ideas in dialogue or conflict |

### The "Fate" Cell: Development in Action

The short-short-short-LONG cell from Symphony 5 is the supreme example of a 4-note motif generating an entire symphonic movement.

| Bar Range | Cell State | What Happens |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| 1–4 | Full statement | Cell presented in unison; two-bar pairs |
| 5–20 | Transposed sequence | Cell at new pitch levels, building through the exposition |
| 44–58 | Horn call interrupts | Cell in augmentation — the horn stretches the rhythm |
| 125–195 | Development: compression | Cell compressed to 1-bar, then 2-beat fragments; passed between instruments |
| 196–227 | Dominant pedal | Cell fragments over sustained V; maximum tension before recapitulation |
| 228+ | Recapitulation | Cell returns at full size — the original statement, now loaded with meaning |

## Interval Vocabulary

| Interval | Beethoven's Usage | Frequency |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Minor 2nd | Chromatic neighbor; sighing motif; late-period tension | Moderate → High |
| Major 2nd | Stepwise motion in lyrical themes | High |
| Minor 3rd | Opening leap in minor-key themes | Moderate |
| Major 3rd | Triumphant leaps; horn-call character | Moderate |
| Perfect 4th | Opening gesture (Piano Concerto 4, Emperor) | High |
| Perfect 5th | Heroic; horn-call; open-string resonance | Moderate |
| Octave | Assertive opening gesture; unison power | High |
| Diminished 7th | Dramatic tension; Sturm und Drang passages | Moderate |

Beethoven's themes tend to open with a defining interval (4th, 5th, octave) that carries the theme's character. The opening leap IS the identity.

## Phrase Construction

| Period | Phrase Character | Typical Structure |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Early | Balanced, Classical | 4+4 bar periods; antecedent-consequent; Haydnesque |
| Middle | Extended, irregular | 5+7, 6+10; phrase elisions; expansion through sequence |
| Late | Fragmented or endless | Phrases dissolve into each other; variation = continuous transformation |

### Middle-Period Phrase Expansion

Beethoven's middle period expands Classical phrase structure by inserting developmental passages where a normal composer would cadence.

```abc
X:2
T:Beethoven Phrase Expansion (Middle Period)
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:C
%% 4-bar phrase starts normally
C2E2G2c2|B2A2 G4|
%% Expected cadence at bar 4 — instead, sequence extends
A2c2e2a2|g2f2 e4|
%% NOW the cadence arrives at bar 6 — expanded from 4 to 6
d2f2e2d2|c8|
```

## Late-Period Melodic Radicalism

| Feature | Description | Example Works |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Trill as melody | Extended trills (4–8 bars) become the thematic surface | Op. 111 Arietta, Op. 109 finale |
| Recitative in instruments | Speech-like, unmeasured melodic passages | Op. 31/2 (Tempest), Op. 110 Arioso |
| Hymn simplicity | Deliberately plain, chorale-like melodies beneath complex variation | Op. 132 "Heiliger Dankgesang" |
| Fugue subject as melodic argument | The fugue subject carries the emotional weight | Grosse Fuge Op. 133, Hammerklavier fugue |
| Extreme register melody | Themes placed in the highest or lowest register, beyond comfortable range | Op. 111, Op. 106 |

```abc
X:3
T:Late Beethoven — Hymn Simplicity (Op. 132 character)
M:4/4
L:1/4
K:F Lydian
V:1 name="Violin I"
V:2 name="Violin II"
%% Deliberately simple; the beauty is in the plainness
[V:1] F2 G2|A2 B2|c4|
[V:2] C2 E2|F2 G2|A4|
```

## Melodic Continuity Across Movements

Beethoven sometimes links movements through shared motivic material — the cell from one movement reappears transformed in another.

| Connection Type | Description | Example |
|-----------------|-------------|---------|
| Cyclic motif | Same cell in multiple movements | Symphony 5: the "fate" rhythm appears in all four movements |
| Attacca transition | Melodic line bridges movements without pause | Symphony 5 mvt. 3→4; Op. 57 mvt. 2→3 |
| Thematic recall | Earlier theme quoted in finale | Symphony 9: all three earlier themes recalled before "Ode to Joy" |
| Gradual emergence | Theme built up gradually across the movement | Ninth Symphony finale: the Joy theme assembles from fragments |
