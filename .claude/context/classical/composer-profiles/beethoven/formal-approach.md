# Ludwig van Beethoven — Formal Approach

Beethoven does not use sonata form — he fights it. The form is not a container; it is an opponent, a constraint to be tested, expanded, and ultimately overcome. Where Haydn uses form with wit and Mozart with grace, Beethoven uses it with will. His codas are not afterthoughts — they are the real climax. His developments are not diversions — they are the dramatic center. His recapitulations are not repetitions — they are transformations.

---

## Forms and Mastery

| Form | Mastery | Beethoven's Signature | Key Works |
|------|---------|----------------------|-----------|
| Sonata form | Definitive — redefined the genre | Form as drama; coda as culmination; development as the center of gravity | Symphonies 3–9, Piano Sonatas Op. 2–111 |
| Variation | Masterful — late-period supreme form | Theme dissolved into unrecognizable transformations; variation as spiritual journey | Diabelli Variations, Op. 109/111 finales, "Eroica" Variations |
| Scherzo | Invented (from minuet) | Faster, rougher, more rhythmically violent than the courtly minuet | Symphonies 3–9, Piano Sonatas |
| Fugue | Late-period obsession | Not Baroque revival but modernized: fugue as emotional struggle | Grosse Fuge, Hammerklavier finale, Op. 131 |
| Rondo | Excellent | Transformed from simple alternation to dramatic narrative | Piano Concertos 3–5, Violin Concerto finale |
| Song form (ABA) | Masterful | Slow movements of great depth and simplicity | Pathetique slow mvt, Op. 132 "Heiliger Dankgesang" |
| Through-composed | Late period | Form emerges from content; no predetermined structure | Op. 131 (7 movements attacca), Ninth Symphony finale |

## Sonata Form: How Beethoven Inhabits It

### The Standard vs. Beethoven

| Section | Classical Standard | Beethoven's Transformation |
|---------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| Introduction | Optional; slow, stately | Can be thematic (Symphony 7); or dramatically integrated (Op. 13) |
| Exposition PT | Theme in tonic; 4–8 bars | Theme may be massive (Eroica: 15 bars) or a bare motif (Sym. 5: 4 notes) |
| Transition | Modulates to V or III | Extended; may be developmental; builds to secondary key with drama |
| Exposition ST | Contrasting theme in new key | Genuine contrast — lyrical vs. rhythmic; sometimes multiple ST themes |
| Closing | Cadential, confirming new key | Can be thematically important; sometimes references PT |
| Development | Works through themes in various keys | Massively expanded; escalating sequences; false recapitulation; the dramatic center |
| Recapitulation | Themes return in tonic | Transformed — not literal repetition; new orchestration, new dynamics, new meaning |
| Coda | Brief confirmation of tonic | Expanded to a "second development" — the real climax of the movement |

### The Coda Revolution

Before Beethoven, codas were brief confirmations. Beethoven turns the coda into the movement's true culmination.

| Work | Coda Length (bars) | What Happens |
|------|-------------------|-------------|
| Symphony 3, mvt. 1 | ~140 bars | New theme introduced; second development; triumphant resolution |
| Symphony 5, mvt. 4 | ~80 bars | Extended presto; tonic hammered home with massive force |
| Symphony 8, mvt. 1 | ~70 bars | Developmental coda longer than the development itself |
| Op. 57, mvt. 1 | ~30 bars | Tempo accelerates (Piu allegro); coda IS the climax |

## The Scherzo — Beethoven's Invention

Beethoven transforms the elegant minuet into something fast, rhythmically driven, and often menacing.

| Feature | Minuet (Haydn/Mozart) | Scherzo (Beethoven) |
|---------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Tempo | Moderate (Allegretto) | Fast (Allegro vivace or Presto) |
| Character | Courtly, elegant, dance-like | Rough, rhythmic, sometimes threatening |
| Meter feel | Graceful triple meter | Triple meter but ONE beat per bar |
| Dynamics | Moderate contrasts | Extreme sfz on unexpected beats |
| Trio | Contrasting character | Often pastoral or lyrical; maximum contrast with scherzo |
| Humor | Haydn-style wit | Rougher: displacement, sudden silences, rhythmic tricks |

```abc
X:2
T:Beethoven Scherzo Character — One Beat Per Bar
M:3/4
L:1/4
K:Cm
%% Scherzo: one impulse per bar; rhythmic drive
!ff!C3|_E3|G3|c2 z|
!p!C3|_E3|!sfz!^F3|G2 z|
```

## Development Sections — The Engine of Drama

| Principle | How Beethoven Applies It |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Escalation | Every sequential repetition is louder, higher, harmonically more intense |
| Compression | Motif presented in 4 bars → 2 bars → 1 bar → fragments (see composition-guide.md) |
| False recapitulation | Theme returns in the wrong key; development resumes; real recap arrives with greater force |
| Dominant preparation | Extended V pedal (8–30 bars) before recapitulation — the held breath |
| New material | Occasionally introduces new themes in development (Eroica coda) |

## Variation Form — Late Period Supreme Form

Late Beethoven finds in variation form what sonata form cannot offer: the capacity for limitless transformation.

| Variation Principle | Beethoven's Approach |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| Theme | Simple, often hymn-like — simplicity is the point |
| Early variations | Close to theme; ornamental; recognizable |
| Middle variations | Character transformations (march, fugue, recitative) |
| Late variations | Theme barely recognizable; trills, filigree, rhythmic dissolution |
| Final variation | Return to simplicity — or radical transcendence |

### Beethoven vs. Mozart Variation

| Aspect | Mozart | Beethoven |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Theme relationship | Each variation recognizably close | Variations progressively depart; late ones unrecognizable |
| Character range | Elegant variety within style | Extreme: march, fugue, recitative, chorale, ecstatic trill |
| Cumulative arc | Pleasant sequence | Spiritual journey — from simple to transcendent |

## Fugue in Beethoven — Not Baroque, Not Academic

| Feature | Baroque Fugue (Bach) | Beethoven Fugue |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| Purpose | Systematic exploration of a subject | Emotional struggle; the counterpoint strains |
| Texture | Clear, balanced voices | Dense, sometimes brutal; voices collide |
| Temperament | Intellectual delight | Psychological drama |
| Resolution | Tonic pedal, trill, cadence | Hard-won; sometimes the struggle never fully resolves |

```abc
X:3
T:Beethoven Fugue Character — Struggle, Not Display (Grosse Fuge character)
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:Bb
V:1 name="Voice 1"
V:2 name="Voice 2" clef=bass
%% Dense, strained — voices clash intentionally
[V:1] B2 c2 d2 _e2|f2 _e2 d2 c2|
[V:2] z8|B,2 c2 d2 _e2|
```

## Multi-Movement Architecture

| Principle | Beethoven's Innovation |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Cyclic connection | Movements share motivic material (Symphony 5) |
| Attacca | Movements connected without pause (Sym. 5 mvt. 3→4, Waldstein mvt. 2→3) |
| Progressive tonality | Entire work moves from one key to another (Sym. 5: C minor → C major) |
| Weight distribution | Finale may be the longest, most complex movement (Sym. 9, Eroica) |
| Slow movement depth | Adagios as philosophical centers, not interludes (Op. 132, Sym. 7) |
