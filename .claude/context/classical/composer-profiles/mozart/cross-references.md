# W.A. Mozart — Cross-References & Influences

## Position in the Tradition

Mozart stands at the apex of Classical style — the point where Italian melody, German counterpoint, French elegance, and Enlightenment humanism fuse into a single voice. He inherited the galant style from J.C. Bach, learned sonata thinking from Haydn, and passed a completed harmonic and formal language to Beethoven, who would shatter it into Romanticism. Every Classical-period technique reaches its most refined expression in Mozart.

## Who Influenced Mozart

| Source | What Was Absorbed | Where You Hear It |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| J.C. Bach | Singing allegro, galant keyboard style, concerto form | Piano concertos — the entire genre's DNA comes from the London meeting (1764) |
| Haydn | Sonata-form development, motivic work, quartet texture | "Haydn" Quartets K.387-465; symphonic development sections |
| Italian opera (Jommelli, Piccini, Sarti) | Vocal melody as supreme value; aria forms; buffa pacing | Every melody Mozart wrote — instrumental music IS vocal music |
| Mannheim School (Stamitz, Cannabich) | Orchestral crescendo, dynamic contrast, wind independence | Paris Symphony K.297 onward; wind writing in concertos |
| Bach & Handel (via van Swieten, 1782) | Fugal technique, contrapuntal density, choral grandeur | Jupiter Symphony finale, Requiem, late keyboard fugues |
| Padre Martini | Strict counterpoint, church style (stile antico) | Mass settings, Requiem fugues, K.387 fugal finale |
| C.P.E. Bach | Empfindsamer Stil — sudden dynamic changes, recitative-like passages | Fantasy in C minor K.475; development section surprises |
| French culture | Elegance, dance forms, orchestral refinement | Minuets, serenades, ballet music in operas |

## Who Mozart Influenced

| Successor | What They Took | Examples |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Beethoven | Sonata-form drama, piano concerto model, motivic integration | Piano Concerto No. 3 (modeled on K.491); sonata development |
| Schubert | Lyric melody, harmonic coloring, song-like instrumental themes | Schubert's Bb Sonata D.960 echoes K.595's farewell tone |
| Mendelssohn | Classical clarity, elegant orchestration, chamber transparency | Octet, Violin Concerto — Mozartian balance updated |
| Brahms | Formal proportion, thematic economy, motivic development | Piano concertos inherit Mozart's dialogue principle |
| Tchaikovsky | Operatic melody in orchestral music, emotional directness | Tchaikovsky adored Mozart; echoes in Romeo and Juliet's lyricism |
| Richard Strauss | Operatic vocal writing, wind orchestration | Der Rosenkavalier — explicitly Mozartian opera |
| Stravinsky | Neoclassical works directly channel Mozart's clarity | The Rake's Progress — 20th-century opera in Mozartian form |

## Comparative Table: Classical Masters

| Dimension | Mozart | Haydn | Beethoven (early) |
|-----------|--------|-------|-------------------|
| **Primary melody source** | Vocal — every line could be sung | Motivic — short cells developed extensively | Motivic + dramatic — themes are pronouncements |
| **Harmonic language** | Diatonic with surgical chromatic inflection | Diatonic with surprise remote modulations | Increasingly chromatic; mode mixture as structural force |
| **Development technique** | Fragmentation + reharmonization; thematic dialogue | Monothematic; one theme in many keys | Motivic saturation; themes broken to atoms and rebuilt |
| **Texture** | Transparent, chamber-like; instruments are characters | Varies: quartet conversations, orchestral wit | Thickening toward orchestral mass; weight and power |
| **Emotional range** | Full spectrum within classical proportion | Wit, surprise, folk humor, occasional depth | Drama, heroism, struggle; emotional extremes |
| **Orchestration** | Wind independence; clarinet love; operatic transparency | String-dominated; witty instrument assignments | Growing orchestral weight; brass/timpani prominence |
| **Form** | Operatic dramatic logic within Classical frames | Experimental: false recaps, monothematic sonata | Expanding forms; codas as second developments |
| **Cadences** | Clear, proportionate, operatically timed | Often delayed or subverted for humor | Extended, emphatic, sometimes delayed for drama |
| **Minor mode** | Shadow within major; G minor as personal key | Sturm und Drang (1770s); otherwise rare | Structural force; C minor as heroic battleground |
| **Humor** | Operatic wit, character comedy, irony | Musical jokes, false endings, metric tricks | Scherzo energy, rhythmic displacement, sforzandi |

## Key Relationships

### Mozart and Haydn: Mutual Teaching

The influence ran both ways. Haydn taught Mozart quartet technique and motivic development; Mozart taught Haydn operatic melody and orchestral color.

| What Haydn Gave Mozart | What Mozart Gave Haydn |
|------------------------|----------------------|
| Monothematic development (Op. 33) | Chromatic richness (after hearing Mozart's quartets) |
| Quartet as conversation | Wind writing independence (London symphonies) |
| Surprise as structural device | Lyric slow movement depth |
| Motivic economy | Operatic emotional range in instrumental music |

[V:2] G,4 D,4|F,4 C,4|G,4 D,4|
%% Same ascending-third gesture transposed upward — Haydn's technique, Mozart's melody
```

### J.C. Bach to Mozart: The Singing Allegro

The 8-year-old Mozart met J.C. Bach in London (1764) and absorbed a fundamental principle: the fast movement can sing. Before this, allegros were energetic but not lyrical. J.C. Bach's galant style — graceful, vocal, keyboard-centric — became the foundation of Mozart's concerto melodies.

| J.C. Bach Trait | How Mozart Transformed It |
|-----------------|-------------------------|
| Galant melody, simple accompaniment | Deepened harmony, enriched accompaniment textures |
| Keyboard concerto as entertainment | Piano concerto as psychological drama |
| Charming surface, no depth | Surface charm concealing emotional complexity |
| Convention-bound form | Form as dramatic argument |

### Mozart to Beethoven: Inheritance and Rebellion

Beethoven revered Mozart and studied with him (briefly, 1787). The early Beethoven concertos and sonatas are explicitly Mozartian — then Beethoven shatters the vessel.

| What Beethoven Inherited | How Beethoven Transformed It |
|-------------------------|------------------------------|
| Piano concerto structure (K.466 -> Concerto No. 3) | Extended orchestra's role; made soloist more combative |
| Sonata-form proportion | Expanded development; coda as second development |
| Chromatic inflection (minor shadow) | Mode mixture as permanent structural force |
| Operatic drama in instrumental music | Epic, symphonic drama — orchestra as protagonist |
| Economy of means | Motivic saturation — carried further than Mozart imagined |

[V:1] C2_E2 G2c2|_B2_A2 G2F2|
[V:2] C,4 C,4|C,4 C,4|
%% Beethoven heard this and made it the foundation of his C minor identity
```

### Italian Opera to Mozart to German Opera

Mozart's unique position: trained in Italian opera seria and buffa, he synthesized both into a new art form where comedy contains tragedy, ensembles reveal psychology, and the orchestra comments on what the characters cannot say.

| Italian Opera Convention | Mozart's Transformation |
|-------------------------|------------------------|
| Aria as vocal display | Aria as character revelation |
| Recitative as connective tissue | Accompanied recitative as dramatic event |
| Ensemble as simple combination | Ensemble as simultaneous psychology — each character has distinct music |
| Overture unrelated to opera | Overture foreshadows the drama (Don Giovanni) |
| Orchestra accompanies | Orchestra comments, contradicts, reveals the subtext |
