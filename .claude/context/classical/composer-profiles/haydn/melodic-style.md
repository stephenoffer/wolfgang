# Joseph Haydn — Melodic Style

Haydn's melodies are deceptively simple. A scale fragment, a repeated note, a folk-song leap — they sound like anyone could have written them. Their genius reveals itself in what happens next: the theme turns out to be infinitely divisible, each fragment generating new material in development. The simplicity is not laziness — it is structural strategy. A complex theme cannot be fragmented; a simple one can be turned inside out.

---

## Core Melodic Characteristics

| Feature | Description | Presence |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Folk-like simplicity | Themes built from scales, triads, repeated notes — singable, memorable | Constant |
| Motivic economy | Entire movements generated from a 2-4 note cell | Very high |
| Hidden sophistication | Simple surface conceals infinite developmental potential | Constant |
| Monothematic construction | "Second theme" is often the first theme in a new key | Very high |
| Phrase-structure wit | Regular 4-bar phrases disrupted by extension, compression, or elision | High |
| Theme as raw material | The theme exists TO BE developed — its beauty is secondary to its function | High |
| Rhythmic identity | Themes defined more by rhythm than pitch contour | High |

## Melodic Construction Principles

### The Art of the Simple Theme

Haydn builds themes from the most basic materials — precisely because basic materials are the most versatile in development.

| Material | Why Haydn Uses It | Development Potential |
|----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Scale fragment (4-5 notes) | Universally recognizable | Can be inverted, retrograded, fragmented to 2 notes |
| Repeated note with rhythm | Rhythmic identity survives any transformation | Fragment to just the rhythm; transpose freely |
| Triadic outline | Establishes key immediately | Reharmonize in any key; the same notes mean different things |
| Folk-song interval (3rd, 5th) | Warmth, accessibility | Audiences feel they already know it |
| Silence within the theme | Creates expectation | The gap becomes a structural device in development |

```abc
X:1
T:Haydn Theme Types — Simple Materials, Infinite Potential
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:G
%% Type 1: Scale fragment (Symphony 104)
G2A2B2c2|d4 B2G2|A2B2c2A2|B4 z4|
%% Type 2: Repeated note with rhythmic identity (Symphony 101 "Clock")
K:D
D2D2 D2F2|A4 A2F2|D2D2 D2A2|d4 z4|
```

## Theme Fragmentation Process

The core of Haydn's melodic genius: a theme is stated complete, then systematically reduced to its smallest components, each fragment becoming the engine of development.

| Stage | What Happens | Length | Example (from 8-bar theme) |
|-------|-------------|--------|---------------------------|
| 1. Complete statement | Full theme presented | 8 bars | Bars 1-8: the whole melody |
| 2. Half theme | First phrase only | 4 bars | Bars 1-4 repeated, varied |
| 3. Head motif | Opening gesture | 2 bars | Bars 1-2 sequenced through keys |
| 4. Core cell | Smallest recognizable unit | 1 bar or less | Bar 1 only, inverted, augmented |
| 5. Rhythmic skeleton | Just the rhythm, new pitches | Any | Rhythm of bar 1 on new notes |

```abc
X:2
T:Haydn Fragmentation — Theme Reduced to Its DNA
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:C
%% Full theme (8 bars compressed to 4 for illustration)
C2E2 G2c2|B2A2 G4|F2A2 c2f2|e2d2 c4|
%% Fragment: first 2 bars only, sequenced down
C2E2 G2c2|B2A2 G4|B,2D2 F2B2|A2G2 F4|
%% Core cell: just the first 4 notes, rapid sequence
C2E2 B,2D2|A,2C2 G,2B,2|
```

## Interval Vocabulary

| Interval | Typical Usage | Frequency |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| Steps (2nds) | Scale-based themes; running passages | Very high |
| 3rds | Triadic themes; folk character | Very high |
| 4ths / 5ths | Opening leaps — assert key, grab attention | High |
| 6ths | Expressive leaps in slow movements | Moderate |
| Octave | Dramatic opening gestures (London symphonies) | Moderate |
| Diminished intervals | Sturm und Drang only; tension and anguish | Low |

Haydn's intervals tend toward the diatonic and consonant. Chromaticism in melody is rare — it belongs to the harmony, not the tune.

## Phrase Structure and Wit

### Regular vs. Irregular Phrasing

Haydn establishes regular 4-bar phrases as a norm, then plays with the listener's expectations by extending, compressing, or interrupting them.

| Technique | How It Works | Comic/Dramatic Effect |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Phrase extension | 4-bar phrase becomes 5 or 6 bars via deceptive cadence | "Not done yet" — the sentence runs on |
| Phrase compression | Expected 4 bars compressed to 3 | Arrives too early — breathless, surprising |
| Phrase elision | New phrase begins on last note of old phrase | No breathing room — continuous forward motion |
| False ending | Theme reaches apparent cadence, then continues | "Oh, there's more?" |
| Silence as phrase | A rest where a note was expected | The gap speaks louder than any note |

```abc
X:3
T:Haydn Phrase Wit — Extension via Deceptive Cadence
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:G
%% Regular 4-bar phrase heading toward cadence...
G2B2 d2B2|c2A2 F2D2|E2G2 B2G2|
%% Expected PAC... but deceptive cadence! (V-vi)
D4 E4|
%% Extra bars to reach the real cadence
D2F2 A2F2|G4 z4|
```

## Monothematic Melody

In many sonata-form movements, Haydn's "second theme" is the first theme transplanted to the dominant. This is not laziness — it demonstrates that harmonic context, not melodic invention, creates contrast.

| Movement | First Theme Key | "Second Theme" | Relationship |
|----------|----------------|---------------|-------------|
| Symphony 88/i | D major | A major | Same theme, dominant key |
| Symphony 104/i | D major | A major | Theme transformed rhythmically |
| Quartet Op. 33/3 | C major | G major | Theme inverted and shortened |
| Symphony 94/i | G major | D major | Theme with new accompanimental texture |

---

## Slow Movement Melody

Haydn's slow movements reveal the lyrical side concealed by the wit of fast movements. Here, themes breathe, expand, and ornament themselves.

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Singing quality | Themes are genuinely vocal — could be arias |
| Ornamental variation | Theme returns with embellishments (turns, trills, passing tones) |
| Wider intervals | 6ths and octaves appear; expressive leaps |
| Longer phrases | 8-bar phrases expand to 10-12 bars |
| Dynamic nuance | pp to p gradations; intimate, not dramatic |

```abc
X:4
T:Haydn Slow Movement Melody — Lyrical, Ornamental
M:3/4
L:1/16
K:F
%% Simple theme, first statement
F4 A4 c4|d4 c4 B4|A4 G4 F4|G8 z4|
%% Same theme, ornamented return
F4 A2G2A2F2 c4|d4 c2B2c2A2 B4|A4 G2A2B2G2 F4|G8 z4|
```

## Finale Melodies

Haydn's finales are typically the wittiest movements — fast, light, rhythmically driven, with themes that lend themselves to comic timing.

| Finale Type | Theme Character | Tempo | Structural Wit |
|-------------|----------------|-------|---------------|
| Rondo | Catchy, folk-like refrain | Presto/Vivace | Returns in unexpected harmonic contexts |
| Sonata-rondo | Rondo theme + developmental episodes | Allegro | Combines wit of rondo with drama of sonata |
| Variation | Simple theme, increasingly elaborate | Andante-Presto | Final variation often changes tempo or mode |
| Fugal | Subject designed for stretto and inversion | Allegro | Op. 20 quartets — contrapuntal virtuosity as climax |

```abc
X:5
T:Haydn Finale Theme — Folk-Like Rondo (Presto character)
M:2/4
L:1/16
K:D
D2F2A2d2|c2B2A2G2|F2E2D2^C2|D4 z4|
A2F2D2A,2|B,2D2F2A2|G2F2E2D2|D4 z4|
```
