# Haydn Idiomatic Device Catalog

Companion to `haydn-lh-vocabulary.md`. The organising fact: **Haydn's music has a
sense of humour and a sense of surprise**, and both are structural rather than
decorative. He sets up an expectation with great clarity and then does not meet
it. A piece in this style with no surprise in it is generic Classical writing.

The second organising fact: **monothematic sonata form.** Haydn frequently uses
the same theme for the first and second subjects, in different keys. The contrast
comes from key and texture, not from a new tune.

## Structural surprises

1. **The general pause** — everything stops mid-phrase, for a beat or a whole bar,
   then the music resumes. His signature, and it appears in most fast movements.

2. **The false recapitulation** — the theme returns in the wrong key, is allowed
   to continue for a few bars, and is then corrected. The listener is caught out
   twice: once by the early return and once by its being wrong.

3. **The extra bar** — a four-bar phrase becomes five because one bar is repeated,
   or seven because the cadence is delayed. Hyper-regular phrase rhythm is not
   his; irregularity is.

4. **The repeated cadence** — a phrase cadences, then cadences again, then a third
   time, each slightly different. Comic when fast, emphatic when slow.

5. **The sudden dynamic** — one bar of `pp` in the middle of a forte passage, or a
   `ff` chord after a long quiet stretch (the "Surprise" gesture, and it works
   because everything before it is regular).

6. **Ending on the wrong thing** — a movement that stops on a dominant, or on a
   fragment, or simply trails off. He does not always close firmly.

7. **The wrong-key opening** — beginning somewhere other than the tonic and
   arriving at it a few bars in.

## Melodic and textural devices

8. **Monothematicism** — the second subject is the first subject in the dominant.
   Write one good theme and re-use it rather than inventing a second.

9. **Motivic development from a tiny cell** — like Beethoven, but wittier: the
   fragment is turned over, inverted, and handed between the hands.

10. **Two-part texture** — melody and a single bass line, no chords at all, for
    whole phrases. Far more common in Haydn than in Mozart and one of the
    cheapest ways to make a texture change.

11. **Folk-derived material** — drone fifths, modal inflections, and dance
    rhythms, especially in finales. The rustic register is deliberate.

12. **Hand crossing** — the left hand plays above the right for a bar or two. A
    colour effect and unmistakably his.

13. **Unison passages** — both hands on the same line for a bare, attention-getting
    statement, usually at a development's opening.

14. **Ornament as punctuation** — a turn or a trill placed at the *end* of a
    gesture rather than on a long note, closing it off like a spoken inflection.

15. **The rhetorical question** — a phrase that ends on a rising interval, on the
    dominant, unanswered, followed by a pause.

## Harmonic devices

16. **Remote modulation by common tone** — one note is held while the harmony
    around it changes into an unexpected key.

17. **Deceptive cadence used comically** — the resolution avoided and then
    immediately made fun of by repetition.

18. **Long dominant preparation, then the theme arrives early** — the setup and
    the payoff deliberately mismatched.

19. **Minore variation** — in a variation set, one variation in the parallel
    minor, usually more chromatic and more serious than its neighbours.

20. **Sudden chromatic side-step** in an otherwise diatonic passage, held for one
    bar, then corrected.

## What to avoid

- **Unrelieved Alberti bass.** The fastest way to write Mozart by accident.
- **Perfect four-bar regularity.** The style depends on setting up regularity and
  then breaking it — but the breaking has to happen.
- **Solemnity throughout.** Even the slow movements have wit.
- **A second theme that contrasts strongly with the first.** Often more
  Mozartian than Haydnesque; consider re-using the first.
- **No silence.** Haydn uses more of it than any other Classical composer.
