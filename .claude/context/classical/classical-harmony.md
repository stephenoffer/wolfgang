# Classical Harmony Reference

## Functional Harmony (T-PD-D-T)

### Function Groups

| Function | Chords (major key) | Chords (minor key) | Role |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|----|
| Tonic (T) | I, vi, iii | i, VI, III | Stability, rest |
| Pre-Dominant (PD) | ii, IV, vi | ii*, iv, VI | Tension preparation |
| Dominant (D) | V, vii* | V, vii* | Maximum tension |
| Tonic (T) | I | i | Resolution |

### Standard Progressions by Function

| Pattern | Chords | Usage |
|---------|--------|-------|
| T-D-T | I-V-I | Basic cadential motion |
| T-PD-D-T | I-IV-V-I | Standard phrase |
| T-PD-D-T | I-ii6-V-I | Stronger pre-dominant |
| T-PD-D-T | I-IV-V7-I | With dominant 7th |
| T-PD-D-T | I-ii6/5-V-I | Smooth bass voice leading |
| Extended | I-vi-IV-ii-V-I | Full functional circuit |


## Harmonic Expression in Classical Style

Classical harmony draws its expressive power from clarity and restraint. When everything is diatonic, a single chromatic note can devastate. The sentence and period structures function as harmonic question-and-answer: a half cadence poses the question (the listener feels incompleteness), and the perfect authentic cadence answers it (the listener feels satisfaction, closure). This is not abstract structure — it mirrors the way human conversation, breathing, and expectation work.

### Structural Function as Emotional Experience

| Structural Function | Harmonic Effect | What the Listener Feels |
|--------------------|----------------|------------------------|
| Antecedent phrase (HC) | Open ending on V | Incompleteness — a question hanging in the air |
| Consequent phrase (PAC) | Closed ending on I | Satisfaction — the question answered, tension released |
| Exposition (tonic area) | Stable home key | Security, grounding, identity established |
| Development section | Remote keys, sequences, fragmentation | Disorientation, adventure — harmonic journey into the unknown |
| Recapitulation | Return to tonic | Homecoming — the relief of return feels earned by the journey |
| Deceptive cadence (V-vi) | Expected resolution denied | Surprise; the story is not finished yet |
| Mode mixture (iv, bVI) | Shadow of parallel minor | A cloud passing over sunlight — Mozart's devastating contrast within diatonic texture |

### The Power of Restraint

Consider Mozart's sudden shift to minor within an otherwise diatonic texture: because the language is so clear, even a single borrowed chord (iv or bVI) creates the effect of shadow entering the light. In Beethoven, mode mixture intensifies — bVI and bVII become characters in the drama, not just coloristic visitors. The harmonic restraint of Classical style is itself an expressive choice: it makes every departure meaningful.

```abc
X:50
T:Classical Harmonic Question and Answer
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:C
% Antecedent - ends on half cadence (question)
C2E2 G2c2 | B2A2 G4 |
% Consequent - ends on PAC (answer)
C2E2 G2c2 | d2B2 c4 |
% Mode mixture - shadow enters (iv in major)
[CF_A]4 [CEG]4 |
```

The development section's harmonic adventure — passing through remote keys — creates a sense of disorientation that makes the recapitulation feel like homecoming. The listener does not merely hear the return of the tonic; the listener feels relief, recognition, and the emotional weight of the journey that preceded it.

## Period and Sentence Structure

### Period

| Part | Bars | Cadence | Function |
|------|------|---------|----------|
| Antecedent | 4 (or 8) | HC or IAC | Question |
| Consequent | 4 (or 8) | PAC | Answer |

### Sentence

| Part | Bars | Content |
|------|------|---------|
| Presentation (basic idea) | 2 | Motive stated |
| Presentation (repetition) | 2 | Motive repeated (same or transposed) |
| Continuation | 2 | Fragmentation, sequence, acceleration |
| Cadential | 2 | Cadential progression |

### Sentence ABC Example (C major)
```abc
X:2
T:Classical Sentence Structure
M:4/4
L:1/8
K:C
% Basic idea (bars 1-2)
C2E2 G2c2 | B2A2 G4 |
% Repetition (bars 3-4, transposed up)
D2F2 A2d2 | c2B2 A4 |
% Continuation + fragmentation (bars 5-6)
G2A2 B2c2 | A2B2 c2d2 |
% Cadential (bars 7-8)
e2d2 c2B2 | c8 |
```

### Phrase Expansion Techniques

| Technique | Description | Effect |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| Internal expansion | Extra bars within phrase | Broadens phrase |
| Cadential extension | Deceptive -> real cadence | Delays closure |
| Prefix | Introductory bar(s) | Sets up phrase |
| Suffix (codetta) | Post-cadential material | Confirms closure |
| Evaded cadence | V resolves irregularly | Restarts phrase |
| One more time | Cadential area repeated | Emphasis |

## Harmonic Rhythm Conventions

| Tempo | Typical changes per bar (4/4) | Notes |
|-------|------------------------------|-------|
| Allegro | 1-2 | One chord per bar common |
| Andante | 2-4 | Change on beats 1 and 3 |
| Adagio | 4-8 | Rich harmonic variety |
| Minuet (3/4) | 1-2 per bar | Often 1 per bar |
| Finale (Presto) | 1 per bar | Very fast harmonic rhythm rare |

### Harmonic Rhythm Acceleration

| Location | Harmonic rhythm | Purpose |
|----------|----------------|---------|
| Phrase opening | Slow (1 per bar) | Establish key |
| Phrase middle | Moderate | Build tension |
| Pre-cadential | Fast (every beat or faster) | Drive to cadence |
| Cadence | Slow (V held) | Prepare resolution |

## Mode Mixture (Borrowing from Parallel Key)

In Classical practice, borrowing chords from the parallel minor into major is increasingly common, especially in Beethoven:

| Borrowed chord | From | Sound | Typical context |
|---|---|---|---|
| iv | Parallel minor | Darker plagal | Subdominant coloring |
| bVI | Parallel minor | Warm, dramatic | Deceptive cadences, mediant color |
| bVII | Parallel minor | Modal, "folk" | Pre-dominant, Beethoven |
| bIII | Parallel minor | Bright but unexpected | Exposition key area (Beethoven) |
| viio7/V (from minor) | Parallel minor | Urgency | Cadential intensification |
| ii° or ii°7 | Parallel minor | Darker ii | Pre-dominant in major-key context |

```abc
M:4/4
L:1/2
K:C

w: I iv(borrowed) i(borrowed) I
```

## Voice-Leading Standards

> **Note:** These reflect common-practice norms (~1750-1820). Beethoven's late works already stretch these conventions, and later periods depart from them deliberately. See period-specific files for guidance on when departures are idiomatic.

| Rule | Classical application |
|------|---------------------|
| Parallel 5ths/8ves | Avoided in classical style |
| Direct 5ths/8ves | Avoided in outer voices |
| Leading tone resolution | Up to tonic (may descend in inner voice) |
| 7th resolution | Down by step |
| Common tone retention | Hold shared pitch between chords |
| Contrary outer voices | Preferred, especially at cadences |
| Spacing | Typically no more than an octave between adjacent upper voices |
| Crossing | Avoid voice crossing |

## Secondary Dominant Chains

## Neapolitan Sixth (bII6)

The Neapolitan sixth characteristically appears in first inversion, lending a rich, darkened color to cadential approaches, especially in minor keys. It functions as a striking pre-dominant harmony.

### Neapolitan Usage Patterns

| Context | Approach | Neapolitan | Resolution | Character |
|---------|----------|------------|------------|-----------|
| Minor cadence | iv or i | bII6 | V or cad 6/4 | Deeply expressive |
| Major key borrowing | IV or ii | bII6 | V | Dramatic darkening |
| Deceptive arrival | After V | bII6 (surprise) | V -> i | Unexpected color |
| Arpeggiated approach | i -> bII6 | Spread chord | V | Operatic, vocal |

### Neapolitan Voice Leading

| Voice | bII6 note (in C minor) | Resolution to V |
|-------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Soprano | F or Ab | E or G |
| Alto | Ab or Db | G or B |
| Tenor | F (bass, first inv.) | G |
| Bass | F (root of bII in inv.) | G |

## Mode Mixture (Borrowed Chords)

Mode mixture introduces chords from the parallel minor into a major-key context, adding emotional depth and shadow. This technique grows increasingly prominent through the Classical period, with Beethoven embracing it extensively.

### Common Borrowed Chords in Major

## Cadential 6/4 — Expanded Usage

The cadential 6/4 is not a root-position tonic chord but a dominant embellishment, where the 6th and 4th above the bass resolve downward to the 5th and 3rd. It characteristically appears on a strong beat, lending weight and anticipation to the cadence.

### Cadential 6/4 Variants

| Variant | Approach | Cad 6/4 | Resolution | Strength |
|---------|----------|---------|------------|----------|
| Standard | ii6 -> | I6/4 -> | V(7) -> I | Full cadence |
| With decoration | IV -> | I6/4 -> | V7 -> I | Broader approach |
| Double | ii6 -> I6/4 -> V -> | I6/4 -> | V7 -> I | Extended, emphatic |
| With Neapolitan | bII6 -> | I6/4 -> | V -> i | Dramatic (minor) |
| Concerto cadenza | Orchestra -> | I6/4 (held) -> | Cadenza -> V -> I | Soloist entry |

## Classical Sequences

Sequences provide the engine of development sections and transitional passages. They transpose a melodic-harmonic pattern systematically, typically through the circle of fifths or by step.

## Modulation via Secondary Dominant + Pivot

Combining secondary dominants with pivot chords enables smooth yet colorful modulations. This characteristically appears at transition points and development openings.

### Modulation Patterns

| From | To | Pivot / Method | Smoothness |
|------|-----|---------------|------------|

| I | vi | V/vi resolves, then treat vi as new i | Smooth |
| I | IV | I becomes V/IV | Direct |
| i | III | V/III resolves to III, establish | Smooth |
| I | bVI | bVI approached via mode mixture | Colorful (Beethoven) |
